ROFL · examples/bleep

BLEEP — blacked out on purpose

A report where the numbers nobody checked are not printed. Not an error message, not a warning: visible dirt, computed by multiplication in a semiring rather than by a subsystem that tracks it.

Explained without jargon.

1 What you are looking at

Here is the rule of this page, and it is the whole idea: if part of an answer came from something nobody checked, the whole answer is blacked out — until somebody works it out a second way, honestly.

Imagine your class works out how much money the school fair made. Most of the numbers come from the till roll, which is a real record. One number — how much was refunded — somebody just remembers hearing in a group chat. Nobody checked it.

The total is worked out from both. So how much do you trust the total? Exactly as much as you trust the shakiest number in it. Not the average. The worst one. A chain is as strong as its weakest link, and this is that, written down as arithmetic.

So the total gets blacked out too. Not because the sum is wrong — the sum is fine — but because you do not know what you added up.

And there is a way to get it back. If somebody works out the same number a second way, using only records you trust, then you have a clean route to that answer and the blacking-out lifts. That is not cheating; it is what checking means.

A quarterly report modelled in ROFL. Numbers arrive through channels with different trustworthiness; the report derives conclusions from them; the question is which conclusions may be shown.

Nothing in bleep.rofl computes trustworthiness. There is no dirty/1, no taint/2, no propagation rule. The rules compute the report, and the levels are the value of each fact under a four-element semiring folded over the support hypergraph the kernel recorded while computing it — evaluateSemiring, the same fold that does counting and provenance for the other examples.

Honestly, about novelty. This is a classical label lattice: Bell–LaPadula, information-flow control, taint tracking. The field is about forty years old and there is no new idea here. What is worth showing is narrower and true — distrust propagation turns out to be multiplication in a semiring, an instance of machinery already present, about fifteen lines in runtime/semirings.ts, rather than the separate subsystem industrial tools implement. That is the entire claim.

2 The report

real output

Five lines print. Nine do not. The right-hand column is the sum that decided it, and it is always the same sum: take the worst. min(clean, dirty) = dirty means "one part was clean, one part was hearsay, so the answer counts as hearsay".

Notice there are two shades of blacking-out. ▒▒▒▒ means "probably fine, but nobody has confirmed it". ████ means "somebody typed this in a chat". ████████ means something different again: that number came from a place we are not allowed to use. Checking it would not help. It is off the table.

Two rows have more than one hyperedge into them, and the column above only shows what ⊕ chose between them: 

  shipping
    via says[main](carrier_invoice,shipping,180000)    min(clean, clean) = clean
    via says[main](vendor_email,shipping,180000)       min(dubious, clean) = dubious
  net_revenue
    via (no licence: a plain step)                     min(clean, clean, dirty) = dirty
    via rule_of_thumb[main](net_revenue,925)           min(dubious, clean, clean) = dubious

shipping is laundering: a supplier's email and the carrier's invoice carry the same number, and max takes the clean route. net_revenue is the same story from the other side — its exact route runs through a chat message and is dirty, its rule-of-thumb route runs through clean inputs but an inexact step and is dubious, and max keeps the better of the two. The report is therefore dubious rather than dirty, which is the correct answer and not one you would guess.

3 The two operations

cleana system of record
dubiousplausible, unreconciled
dirtyhearsay — somebody typed it
forbiddenmay not be used at all

Down a chain, take the worst. If an answer is built out of several things, it is only as good as the shakiest one. One bad ingredient spoils the dish however carefully you cook afterwards. This is the part an ordinary spreadsheet does not do: there the label falls off the moment you add two cells together.

Across different ways of getting there, take the best. If the same answer can also be reached by a route you trust, use that one. You worked it out twice and one of the workings was clean, so the answer is clean. That is not a loophole — it is exactly what "check it" means.

operatorismeaning
⊗min along one derivation: a conclusion is never cleaner than its worst premise
⊕max across alternative derivations: laundering through independent derivation
onecleanan axiom costs nothing
zeroforbiddenadditive identity and annihilator

That last row says something worth saying out loud: a fact whose every route is forbidden is annotated exactly like a fact with no route at all. An embargoed source is algebraically no source. It is also the veto — revenue_per_head is Boolean-derivable, the engine derives it, and its annotation is the semiring zero, so an action gated on a clean basis reads "no admissible derivation" and "no derivation" as the same value. Nothing to tune, nothing to evade.

Why the instance declares BOUNDED

Every instance in runtime/semirings.ts declares its convergence discipline and says why the declaration is true. This one declares BOUNDED, and the argument is a counting argument: the carrier has four elements, iteration starts every derived fact at forbidden and only ever moves it up, and clean is the top. Any fact's value can change at most three times, whatever the support graph does. Cycles are free.

Contrast countingSemiring, which is CLOSED and needs star: ⊕ = + has infinite height, so over a cycle the count really does grow without bound and "infinitely many" has to be a legal carrier value rather than an overflow. Here, going round a cycle can only take min against a value already seen.

(star(a) = one ⊕ a ⊕ a² ⊕ … = max(clean, a) = clean = one, so declaring it CLOSED would compute the same values. BOUNDED is the honest declaration, because convergence is by finite height and not by closure.)

4 The label is on the edge

Here is a distinction that sounds fussy and is the whole trick.

"Someone said in the chat that refunds were 315,000" — that is true. The message exists. You could screenshot it. Nothing about that is doubtful.

"Refunds were 315,000" — that is the doubtful part.

So the doubt does not belong to either sentence. It belongs to the arrow between them: the step from "somebody said it" to "it is so". Put the mark on the arrow, and you can keep the message as a solid fact while treating what it claims as shaky. Put the mark on the message instead and you have to pretend the message never happened in order to doubt it, which is silly.

says(ops_chat, returns, 315000) is a clean fact. The doubtful thing is the step from "the channel said it" to "the number is 315000", and that step is a hyperedge. evaluateSemiring has exactly that slot: weight(key, witness) is called once per firing, and a firing is a hyperedge. demo.ts supplies it; nothing in the model carries a level.

Two consequences. The same fact, two edges, two labels — figure(shipping, 180000) above. And doubt with no premise to hang it on: the rule-of-thumb route to net_revenue has clean premises and an inexact inference, so there is no fact to label.

Where the distinction is real, and where it is not. Being exact, because this is easy to oversell: in a Datalog where every step is licensed by a fact, node labels on those licence facts are expressively equivalent for the values of derived facts. Labelling the says fact dirty would annotate everything downstream identically. Two things are still lost. First, the record is corrupted in order to express doubt about the claim — under node labelling says(ops_chat, …) itself reads dirty, which is false. Second, counterfactuals stop being cheap: "what gets laundered if I verify X" is answered here by re-folding with a different label function over the same store, with no assertion, no retraction and no re-derivation. The fold did not fight the model on any of this; weight(key, witness) is a proper edge slot and it was enough. The trap next door is base(key), which is a node slot — mixing the two is how the distinction quietly gets lost.

5 Four questions

Which link is dirty

Follow the working backwards, and at every step keep the worst part. You end up at the one arrow that spoiled it, and it names the chat.

The kernel's own derivation tree, then the same tree with the lattice folded over it: descend the route ⊕ chose, take its worst part, repeat. The chain ends on an edge, not on a fact.

real output

refund_rate prints as ████. The kernel's own derivation tree:

refund_rate[main](75)  <= r5e0a72f7 @tick 0
  figure[main](returns,315000)  <= r4b8d2a74 @tick 0
    says[main](ops_chat,returns,315000) [axiom]
  figure[main](gross_revenue,4200000)  <= r4b8d2a74 @tick 0
    says[main](billing_export,gross_revenue,4200000) [axiom]
  75 is /(*(315000,1000),4200000) [builtin]

and the same tree with the lattice folded over it, descending the route
(+) chose and taking its worst part at every step:

  refund_rate[main](75)                      dirty      worst premise below
  figure[main](returns,315000)               dirty      EDGE is dirty, licensed by says[main](ops_chat,returns,315000)

What would have to be confirmed

Ask the machine why there is no trustworthy route to that number, and it tells you the exact missing piece: nobody has said the chat is a reliable source, and no reliable source carries this number.

clean_route(M, V) is the structural precondition and nothing more: some system of record carries this number directly. It is deliberately not recursive — propagating along a derivation is ⊗'s job, and writing it a second time in the rules would be the separate subsystem this example exists to avoid.

real output

$ whynot clean_route(returns, 315000)
whynot clean_route[main](returns,315000):
  rule r3f0bfd8e: clean_route[main](?M,?V)@now :- says[main](?S,?M,?V)@now, trust[main](?S,clean)@now
    failed premise: trust[main](ops_chat,clean)
      no rule concludes 'trust' and no matching base fact exists

The failing premise is the whole answer: trust(ops_chat, clean) is what is
missing. Confirm ops_chat, or find a system of record that carries 315000.

And the contrast that shows corroboration is not the same as cleanliness:
  shipping   carried by 2 channel(s) — carrier_invoice, vendor_email — clean_route holds, level clean
  headcount  carried by 1 channel(s) — hr_embargo — clean_route FAILS, level forbidden

The last two lines are what stops corroboration being mistaken for cleanliness. Two dirty channels agreeing gives two dirty edges, and max(dirty, dirty) is still dirty.

Which single check is worth doing

If you only have time to check one thing, which one? The machine works it out rather than guessing: checking the chat un-blacks five lines; checking the supplier un-blacks two.

The inverse query, and the most practical of the four. Verification moves an edge label, so one re-fold over the unchanged store answers it. Verification priority, derived rather than guessed.

real output

  verify ops_chat (dirty)       -> 5 report line(s) clean, 6 facts in all
       returns, net_revenue, operating_profit, margin_pct, refund_rate
  verify vendor_email (dubious) -> 2 report line(s) clean, 4 facts in all
       fx_rate, cogs_eur

Look closely at the second line. The supplier's email does mention the shipping cost — and checking it would still not un-black anything about shipping, because shipping already has a second, trusted source. Effort there buys nothing. Nobody worked that out by hand.

vendor_email carries shipping and confirming it cleans nothing there, because shipping is already laundered. That fell out of the fold. A forbidden channel is not on the list at all: an embargo is a permission, not a doubt.

How many clean routes exist

Zero means there is nothing in the model to check it against — a person has to go and look. Two means the number is confirmed twice over.

Which reading of the count applies here. The project records this as a finding: the same metric reads in different directions by domain, and an instance that ships the number bare invites a tool to report robustness where it should report ambiguity. Across this example set it now reads five ways — robustness (NOPE, OOPS), ambiguity (AKA), fragility (SPAT), and launderability here. The interpretation belongs to the instance, not to the metric.

6 The echo chamber

Two people each say "well, they said so". That is not two sources; it is one rumour going round in a circle. Counting how many times it has been repeated gives you an enormous number and tells you nothing. Taking the worst link every time round the circle gives you the right answer immediately, and the counting never runs away.

ops_chat and vendor_email each restate the other, so stands_behind rests on itself through the loop — a cycle of the support hypergraph, not of the rules only, which is the kind that actually breaks folds.

real output

  counting   stands_behind[main](ops_chat,shipping,180000)
             infinitely many — arithmetically right, epistemically absurd
  trust      dirty — the loop adds nothing, because going round it
             can only take min against a value already seen

The fold declared BOUNDED and stopped after 5 rounds with
16 facts on a cycle and disciplineHeld=true. No closure operator, no depth
cap: a four-element lattice has finite height and every value starts at
forbidden and only ever rises, so the chain has nowhere to run.

Mutual confirmation is not independent confirmation, and the lattice says so without being told. test/example-bleep.test.ts also runs a fixture where every fact is on the cycle, and checks that a round cap far below any runaway gives byte-identical values.
