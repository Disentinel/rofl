JOPA — Judgments Over Provenance Algebra

The same rules, the same facts, and three different answers — because in law how sure you have to be changes with the proceeding. Here that is not written into the rules at all. It is a number you hand to the calculation afterwards.

Explained without jargon.

This is a demonstration of a mechanism. It is not legal advice. The statute below is synthetic — invented for this page, modelled on no country's law. The case is invented. Nothing here decides anybody's claim, and nothing on this page should ever be shown to anyone as if it did.

1 The thing we are asking about

A mill burned down. The owner had insurance, so he asked for his money. The insurance company thinks he set the fire himself — and if he did, the policy says they do not have to pay.

Now the same question — did he do it on purpose? — has to be answered by two different people, and here is the thing most people never notice: they are not allowed to be equally unsure.

  • The insurance company only has to think it is likely.
  • A court deciding whether he goes to prison has to be almost completely certain.

So the very same story can be strong enough to lose you your insurance money and nowhere near strong enough to convict you. That is not a bug in the law. It is the law working the way it is supposed to.

One claim under a synthetic indemnity statute; one set of established facts; two proceedings whose standards of proof differ. The composite element in dispute — deliberate causation of the loss — is the same derived predicate in both, from the same rules over the same case file.

Every existing legal-reasoning engine we know of handles this by baking the threshold into the rule logic or by deciding it outside the engine. Here the standard of proof is a table of facts no rule reads, and switching it amends that table.

What is not claimed. "Datalog for statutes" is a forty-year-old field — Catala, PROLEG, LegalRuleML, Blawx, Sergot & Kowalski from 1986 on. This example claims exactly one thing: that the standard of proof can be a parameter of the algebra rather than a constant in the rules. Nothing else.

The synthetic statute, in full

§what it says
s.2the insured perils are fire, storm and escape of water
s.4where the elements in s.5 are established, the insurer shall indemnify
s.5five elements: cover in force, insured property, insured peril, causation, notice within 30 days
s.6what proves what — authority reports, physical traces, presence; and an anonymous communication is not evidence of any fact
s.7no indemnity where the loss was deliberately caused by the policyholder
s.8the standards of proof — (1) the elements, on the balance; (2) an allegation of deliberate loss, by clear and convincing evidence; (3) an offence under s.9, beyond reasonable doubt
s.9deliberately causing a loss and claiming on it is an offence

That whole statute becomes 12 rules. The smallness is the point: it is the only thing anyone has to check by eye.

2 Three levels of "sure"

Think of one long bar from "no idea" on the left to "completely certain" on the right. The law draws lines on that bar, and a different line counts depending on what is at stake.

Three thresholds, as data. s.8 assigns a standard to a question; the standard has a value. Nothing below reads either table — the demo checks that through the kernel's own reflection of the rules, not by grepping a file. 

standard(balance_of_probabilities, 50).
standard(clear_and_convincing,     75).
standard(beyond_reasonable_doubt,  95).

standard_for(claim_elements, balance_of_probabilities, s8_1).
standard_for(exclusion_s7,   clear_and_convincing,     s8_2).
standard_for(offence_s9,     beyond_reasonable_doubt,  s8_3).
50% — more likely than not 75% — clear & convincing 95% — beyond reasonable doubt

Those three lines are the only thing that changes between the three answers on this page. Not the rules. Not what happened. Just where the line is.

3 What the machine works out

The machine follows the story from the evidence up to the accusation and keeps track of how much certainty survives each step. It ends up with one number for "he did it on purpose":

One fold of the Viterbi semiring over the support hypergraph the engine already recorded: each fact gets the probability of its most probable derivation. The rules do not know this is happening.

The other kind of failure — one a threshold alone would hide

There is a second fire in the file. Same accusation, and the lab evidence is just as good — but the only thing saying the owner was there that night is an unsigned letter, and the law says an anonymous letter is not evidence of anything at all. So it counts for nothing. Not "a little". Nothing.

Claim c_okoro: the only route to at_property runs through evidence the tribunal weighed at 0. Probability zero is an explicit symbol in the carrier, not a float arithmetic produced, so it annihilates the chain above it rather than merely dragging it down.

the allegationvalue1%50%75%95%
deliberate_loss(c_ash) 0.7038 ✓ ✓ × ×
deliberate_loss(c_okoro) impossible × × × ×

Look at the 1% column. Even if you dropped the bar to "one chance in a hundred", the first accusation gets over it and the second still does not. Being almost unproven and being unprovable are two different things, and only the second one stays no matter how low you set the bar.

Both are derivable — the Boolean reading lists both and cannot tell them apart. One allegation is not strong enough; the other cannot be established at any standard above zero, and lowering the standard never rescues it. Excluded evidence is not weak evidence. 

established(c_okoro,accelerant_used)  = 0.8000
established(c_okoro,at_property)      = impossible
deliberate_loss(c_okoro)              = impossible   <- one impossible premise, and the whole chain is impossible

They agree in exactly one place: a standard of 0, which both clear — the algebra saying out loud that a standard of 0 is not a standard. Nothing in this example casts the carrier to a plain number, because a cast would flatten precisely this distinction: "we could not prove it" and "this could never have proved anything" are not the same sentence in a decision about somebody's property or liberty.

Same fire. Same evidence. Same rules. The only difference between the last card and the first is where somebody drew the line — and in one of them the man gets his money and in the other he does not.

The third card is a one-fact amendment to s.8(2), run in the demo. What makes it worth printing is what did not happen: 

    before: exclusion at 75%  -> indemnity payable
    after:  exclusion at 50%  -> indemnity refused: the s.7 exclusion is made out

    derived facts identical:        true
    annotations on them identical:  true

Nothing was recomputed differently. The fixpoint did not move and neither did a single annotation. A rule of the form refuse(C) :- deliberate_loss(C), confidence(C, X), X >= 75. would have required editing the logic of the norm to state the same amendment.

4 Exactly which step stops being sure enough

"Not proven" is a useless answer on its own. The useful answer is which link in the chain gave out — and that is a different link depending on how sure you had to be.

The chain below is the argmax backpointer of the same fold — the most probable derivation, read back through the support the store recorded. A step "breaks" at a standard when it does not clear it although everything it rests on does. Values only fall as a derivation is composed, so that is exactly where confidence dropped below the line.

deliberate_loss(c_ash) 0.7038

s.7 No indemnity is payable where the loss was deliberately caused by the policyholder or with their connivance.

rests on 90% — inference strength s7_step, chosen by the modeller [calibration]

50% ✓ 75% × 95% ×
At 75% the chain breaks here. Both findings of fact below still clear the line. It is the inference from them to a deliberate act that does not.
established(c_ash, at_property) 0.9200

s.6(3) presence at the property may be proved by any admissible evidence

rests on 92% — e_cam_07: still from the yard camera opposite the gate, timed 23:41 [record]

50% ✓ 75% ✓ 95% ×
established(c_ash, accelerant_used) 0.8500

s.6(2) physical traces recovered from the property are evidence of the manner in which a peril was brought about

rests on 85% — e_lab_01: county forensic laboratory, petroleum distillate recovered from three floor samples [record]

50% ✓ 75% ✓ 95% ×
At 95% the break has moved down here. Under the criminal standard the laboratory report does not itself reach the line, and neither does the camera still.

Read the two red boxes. At the middle level the pieces of evidence are each fine and it is the guess joining them together that is too shaky. At the highest level even the lab report on its own is not enough. Those are two completely different complaints, and a machine that just says "not proven" tells you neither.

Note where the weakest number came from. The 90% is not law and not a finding of fact — it is the modeller's, and it lives in its own ledger ([calibration], writer modeller) precisely so that it can be pointed at. If you distrust one number in this whole demonstration, distrust that one.

5 "Does not meet the requirements"

"Your claim does not meet the requirements" is an absent whynot, not an absent ground. The ground exists. It is a specific condition of a specific norm and a specific fact, and the machine that refused you knows all three.

This is the part people have spent years failing to get out of institutions. Not "no". Why no — which rule, which condition, and which fact was missing. Two refusals from the same case file:

Both trees below are produced by the kernel's recursive whynot; the sentences above them are read back out of the same model — the days, the limit, the list of insured perils. Nothing is a template about a claim the engine did not actually decide.

6 Where the model sits

An AI reads the law and turns each sentence into a rule. Then a small, dumb, completely predictable program does the actual reasoning, and prints the chain it followed with the sentence of the law next to every step.

So a human never has to check whether the AI's answer is right. They only have to check whether it copied each sentence over correctly — and that they can do by reading twelve short rules next to twelve short sentences.

  1. a model translates the text of a norm into rules as data;
  2. ROFL computes and emits a derivation tree with citations;
  3. a human checks the translation, not the conclusion.

Hallucination is then possible only at step 1, localised to a specific rule, sitting next to the sentence it claims to translate, and checkable by eye. The positioning is not "an LLM with explainability" but an LLM as a parser in front of an auditable kernel: in the first case the explanation is generated after the fact and may diverge from the real cause; in the second the explanation is the cause. The reasoned decision on this page is the argmax backpointer of the fold. There is no second artefact that could disagree with it.

That is what the norm(S) premise on every operative rule buys: it puts the citation inside the derivation, so the provenance semiring reports it as a source and the test can assert mechanically that no conclusion is reachable without passing through a cited norm.

ledgerholdswriter
[main]the lawlegislature
[record]the facts as found, including the weight put on each piece of evidencetribunal_of_fact
[calibration]how much confidence survives an inference stepmodeller

Three books, three writers, and the kernel's forged[audit] catches a number filed in the wrong one with no checking code written.

7 What this does not show

Everything else in this project can be checked by a second program: run the real thing, compare the answers. This one cannot. Whether the law was copied into rules correctly is something only a lawyer can say, and no amount of computing makes that go away.

So we did not invent a fake judge to mark our homework. What we can check is that the machine is consistent with itself — and we do. What we cannot check, we have written down.

JOPA has no independent oracle. Every other example in the set can be checked against something executable — a shell pipe, an IAM simulator, a spreadsheet, exhaustive enumeration. Correctness of a legal translation is verifiable only by a lawyer. This example therefore stands last, because it lacks an oracle, not because it is hard. Two things partially substitute: the translation is small enough to read in full, and every conclusion carries a tree back to the norm it rests on.

Checked mechanically — 19 tests, about 2 seconds

  • the kernel's seven audits are empty; every rule is range-restricted
  • no rule reads a standard of proof — via the kernel's own reflection of the rules, not a grep
  • moving the standard moves nothing: identical derived facts, identical annotations, different verdict
  • a higher standard is never cleared where a lower one is refused, over every annotated fact
  • no conclusion is reachable without passing through a cited norm
  • impossible is not merely improbable: a weak conclusion fails only the higher standards and clears some positive one; an impossible conclusion fails every standard down to 1e-9 — and both clear a standard of 0, because a standard of 0 is not a standard
  • the weights are live: revise a finding of fact and the conclusion moves
  • three mutation tests break the model by hand — a threshold smuggled into a rule, a conclusion citing no norm, an unreadable weight — and each is caught. A check that has never failed has not been shown to work.

Not established, and not establishable here

  • that the translation is faithful. The statute is synthetic, so there is nothing to be faithful to.
  • that the evidence weights are calibrated. 92% for a camera still is a number a tribunal would have to justify. Here it is invented.
  • that multiplying along a chain is right. It assumes independence; traces and presence are not independent.
  • that max across routes is right. Corroboration does not accumulate here: two independent 60% witnesses are worth 60%, not more. Law thinks corroboration matters; Viterbi cannot say so. There is a test that asserts this limitation rather than hiding it.
  • that the standard belongs on the composite rather than element by element. Five elements at 90% give a composite of 59%; a lawyer would say each element is proved. That is a real dispute in evidence law and the algebra does not settle it — it only makes the choice explicit and movable.
  • anything at all about any real jurisdiction.

Where the kernel fought back

The exclusion in s.7 is not written as a negated premise of s.4, and that is the load-bearing design decision of the whole example. A negated premise is settled in the Boolean fixpoint, which runs before any semiring is chosen — so the exclusion would be in or out at derivability, at no standard of proof at all, and s.8(2)'s higher standard for it could not be stated.

The model ships the demonstration. s.5 is in it twice: as a conjunction of its five elements, and as "no element is missing" with the element list as data. Same verdict on every claim, and: 

elements_met(c_ash)         = 0.9700   (the five elements, multiplied)
elements_met_closed(c_ash)  = 1.000   (nothing is missing -- and a finite failure carries no annotation)

The second reading says the claim is certain. It is not; it rests on a fire brigade report the tribunal put at 97%. So whether a norm can be tested at a standard of proof at all depends on how the norm was written down — which is not a property anyone would expect a translation to have to preserve.
