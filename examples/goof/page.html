GOOF — Grothendieck Or Other Foundations

People spent two thousand years arguing about one rule of geometry. It turned out you can just switch it off — and get a different, perfectly good geometry. This page runs that argument as a program: the same reasoning, nine different starting sets of rules, and the computer works out who ends up believing what.

One rule set, evaluated against nine ledgers. A foundation is a perspective — a truth context with a named list of writers — so thm[euclid](angle_sum_180) and thm[lobachevsky](defect_positive) coexist without a special case. The rules never name a foundation; the switch is which axioms a book holds.

the argument

One stubborn rule, and two thousand years

The fifth postulate is separable, and that is the whole demonstration

Around 300 BC, Euclid wrote down five rules for geometry. Four of them are the sort of thing nobody argues with: you can draw a line between two points; all right angles are the same size. The fifth was different and clumsy, and it comes down to this:

Take a line, and a dot that is not on it. How many lines can you draw through the dot that never touch the first line?

Euclid said: exactly one. And that sounds obvious. For two thousand years mathematicians tried to prove it from the other four rules, because a rule you can prove is not really a rule — it is a result. Nobody managed. Then, in the 1800s, a few people tried assuming the opposite, expecting nonsense — and got a whole new geometry that works perfectly well and is not the one we learn at school.

This program keeps nine "books". Each book is a list of starting rules. The reasoning is written once and shared by all of them. Change one line in one book, and watch what stops being true.

The natural way to model "different foundations" is to make the foundation an argument: thm(euclid, angle_sum_180). That is the mega-main anti-pattern from docs/choosing-perspectives.md: it loses forged, it loses the audit of cross-book reads, and it makes "the rules never mention a foundation" unfalsifiable, because the column is in every rule.

A foundation is a ledger, and it passes the discipline's litmus without argument. Two contradictory facts coexist legitimately under different books — that is the entire subject. A book is chosen, not computed. A book says whose entry this is, not how the entry was obtained. And the writers are nameable: the axiom-setter, via authority(euclid, euclid), plus the deduction rules.

axiom[euclid](post5_unique).        -- Playfair: exactly one parallel
axiom[lobachevsky](post5_many).     -- at least two
axiom[riemann](post5_none).         -- none, and postulate 2 goes too

The rest of this page is what falls out of those three lines and one twenty-eight-clause rule set that mentions none of them.

the library

Nine books, each with its own list of starting rules

Nine ledgers, and what separates them, measured

euclid −300

The geometry from school. One parallel line, angles of a triangle add up to 180.

Four postulates, Archimedes, classical logic, plus Playfair's form of the fifth. The baseline.

saccheri 1733

The same book with the fifth rule simply missing. A priest spent thirty years trying to prove it had to be there.

Neutral geometry: no parallel axiom at all. Everything derivable here is derivable in all three geometries.

lobachevsky 1829

One line changed: many parallels instead of one. Triangles now add up to less than 180, and the bigger they are the less.

Hyperbolic. The defect is positive and proportional to area; similarity collapses into congruence.

riemann 1854

Geometry on a ball. No parallels at all — and, less famously, two lines had to change, not one.

Elliptic. Postulate 2 becomes "unbounded but finite", which takes Euclid I.16 and the whole neutral tower above it.

solid / schlafli 1852

Euclid in three dimensions, and Euclid in four. Everything is the same except one number.

Euclid's list with the dimension axiom swapped. The only difference anything downstream can see is a knot.

brouwer 1908

Euclid's geometry, but you are not allowed to prove something by showing the opposite is impossible.

Euclid minus excluded_middle. Only steps whose proof is a reductio are lost, and the model records which those are.

frege 1893

A book that contradicts itself and takes the usual rule "from a contradiction, anything follows". So it proves everything.

Holds both parallel axioms and ex_falso. All 37 propositions become theorems. Russell's letter, 1902.

dacosta 1963

The same contradiction, but this book refuses the "anything follows" rule. It stays contradictory in one spot and sensible everywhere else.

Paraconsistent: same clashes, no ex_falso. 28 of 37 propositions, and two angle-sum laws held at once.

The surprise in that table. Everyone tells the story as "Riemann changed the one rule about parallels". He had to change two. On a ball, a straight line comes back to where it started — it has no end, but it is not endless. So the rule that says "a line can always be extended" had to go as well. And that second change quietly destroys a pile of results nobody thought were about parallels at all.

Riemann differs by two lines, and that is the story rather than a defect in it. A great circle is unbounded but finite, so post2_extend is replaced by post2_finite. Euclid I.16 — the exterior angle theorem — proves itself by doubling a median, which needs the line to extend; it is false on a sphere, and the whole neutral tower resting on it goes with it. The provenance section below identifies exactly which theorems those are, and five of them never needed the fifth postulate at all.

the classic question

How many lines can you draw through the dot?

One, none, infinitely many — from four rules and a semiring

Here is the question the whole argument was about, asked of every book. Notice that nobody wrote the numbers down anywhere: the program worked them out from the starting rules.

Existence is neutral: Euclid I.31 proves at least one parallel exists using no parallel axiom. The fifth postulate is about the number. Four rules turn the axiom into witnesses, and the last one is the hyperbolic construction — between any two distinct parallels lies another:

parallel[G](sole)     :- thm[G](parallel_exists), axiom[G](post5_unique).
parallel[G](limit_a)  :- thm[G](parallel_exists), axiom[G](post5_many).
parallel[G](limit_b)  :- thm[G](parallel_exists), axiom[G](post5_many).
parallel[G](interior) :- parallel[G](X), parallel[G](Y), X != Y.
   book          exists?  witnesses                          derivations
   euclid        yes      sole                               1
   solid         yes      sole                               1
   saccheri      yes      (none)                             0
   lobachevsky   yes      interior, limit_a, limit_b         infinitely many
   schlafli      yes      sole                               1
   riemann       no       (none)                             0
   frege         yes      interior, limit_a, limit_b, sole   infinitely many
   brouwer       yes      sole                               1
   dacosta       yes      interior, limit_a, limit_b, sole   infinitely many

"Infinitely many" is not a special case somebody typed in. In the book where there are two parallels, the rule says: between any two of them there is another one. So you can keep going forever, and the program notices that the reasoning loops back into itself and reports "infinitely many" instead of counting for ever.

interior sits on a cycle of the support hypergraph, because the construction consumes its own output. The CLOSED counting semiring closes that cycle — star(one) is INFINITE for a count — so the fold terminates with the correct answer rather than diverging. In Euclid the rule never fires: it needs two different parallels and there is one.

Being careful about what was counted. The program counts ways of proving there is another line, not the lines themselves. They match up here because every time you use the rule you get a genuinely new line — but that is something a person has to check, and it is written down rather than assumed.

The count is of derivations, not of lines. They coincide because each application of the construction exhibits a new line strictly between two old ones — a property of this construction, not of counting in general. It is the modelling assumption that carries the word "infinitely many" from the algebra back to the geometry, and it is stated in goof.rofl §4.3 rather than left implicit.

And how do you check that "infinitely many" is a real answer and not the program giving up? Ask it to count only the short proofs, then slightly longer ones, then longer still. If the answer keeps growing, it really is endless:

The positive control. An INFINITE produced by closing a cycle is a claim about the support graph. Test it by refusing to close the cycle: depthBoundedCountingSemiring(n) counts derivations of height at most n — different semantics, declared on the instance.

    derivations of height at most      lobachevsky/interior        euclid/sole
      10                                       0                  1
      15                                     682                  1
      20                                  699050                  1
      25                               715827882                  1

One column grows without settling; the other is fixed by depth 10. The CLOSED instance is not hiding a failure to converge — it is naming one.

Look at saccheri in the table: the book knows there is a parallel line and cannot say how many. That is the right answer, and it is the answer the priest could not accept.

saccheri proves parallel_exists and has no witnesses. Neutral geometry does not determine the number, and the model declines to guess rather than defaulting to Euclid.

the failed proof

Thirty years of work, answered in a millisecond

whynot: a finite failure with a demonstration attached

Girolamo Saccheri believed the fifth rule had to follow from the other four, and that if he assumed the opposite he would eventually hit a contradiction and be done. He published in 1733, convinced. He was wrong, and the reason is small enough to fit in seven lines. Ask the program why the angle-sum result is not provable in his book:

Saccheri's programme was: assume the negation, derive a contradiction, and the fifth postulate follows from the other four. The second rule in the answer below is literally that programme — if the book explodes, everything follows — and it fails because the book does not explode.

whynot thm[saccheri](angle_sum_180):
  rule rac159495: thm[?G](?P)@now :- axiom[?G](?P)@now
    failed premise: axiom[saccheri](angle_sum_180)
  rule r7cb3920d: thm[?G](?P)@now :- explodes[?G](?P)@now
    failed premise: explodes[saccheri](angle_sum_180)
  rule r9749238b: thm[?G](?P)@now :- step[main](?S,?P)@now, holds_from[?G](?S,1)@now
    failed premise: holds_from[saccheri](s_playfair,1)

post5_unique — the fifth rule itself. It is not in his book, nothing else produces it, and that is the end of it.

Beltrami settled it in 1868 by building a model of hyperbolic geometry inside Euclidean geometry, which is a consistency proof rather than a search. Here it is a finite failure, and the demonstration bottoms out on "no rule concludes axiom and no matching base fact exists" — the exact shape of what Saccheri was looking for and could not find.

inside the engine

What the computer is actually doing

The full walkthrough: load, stratify, fire, fold, interrogate

Everything above is one program running once. Here is what happens inside it, step by step. It is worth reading even if you skip the code, because the machine does something unusual: it keeps its reasons. It does not just work out the answers — it remembers, for every answer, exactly which starting rules and which steps produced it, so you can ask "why?" afterwards and get a real answer instead of a shrug.

Step 1 — the books get loaded, each signed by its author

1. Loading: nine sections, nine identities

The file is split into sections, one per book, and each is loaded "as" the person who wrote it. That is not decoration: later, when somebody tries to sneak a rule into a book they do not own, the program notices without anybody writing a check for it.

demo.ts splits goof.rofl on its -- @who X markers and loads each block under that identity. The kernel emits asserted_by(fact, who) and in_perspective(fact, ledger), and boot.rofl's audit is one rule:

forged[audit](F) :- asserted_by(F, Who), in_perspective(F, P), not authority(P, Who).

5351 facts land in the store, of which 1716 are domain facts; the rest are reflection — every rule of the program is itself stored as facts (rule, premise_lit, concludes, reads_from, writes_to), which is what makes the audits below ordinary queries rather than compiler passes.

Step 2 — the program checks itself before it starts

2. The audits, which are rules over the program's own reflection

  ? unstratified(X)                    -> 0 rows
  ? malformed[audit](R)                -> 0 rows
  ? breach[audit](R)                   -> 0 rows
  ? leak[audit](A, B)                  -> 0 rows
  ? forged[audit](F)                   -> 0 rows
  ? unmoded[audit](R)                  -> 0 rows
  ? undefined_premise[audit](R, Rel)   -> 0 rows

All seven checks are clean. An empty check is only worth something if it can also say no, so the section at the bottom of this page plants a real violation and shows this one catching it.

Seven empty audits, and the seventh was not always empty: writing rules polymorphic in the ledger cost a false leak row until this example paid for the kernel repair. The last section tells that story and shows the audit still catching a real leak in the same store, so the empty row is not an alarm somebody switched off.

Step 3 — sorting the work into layers

3. Stratification: the deduction layer needs no negation at all

Some questions can only be answered after others. "What does Euclid's book prove?" has to be settled before "what does Euclid's book have that Riemann's does not?", because the second question involves the word not. The program sorts its rules into layers so that never goes in a circle.

Negation forces a stratum boundary: a rule reading not p may only run once p is complete. Of GOOF's 28 clauses, exactly one uses negation.

stratumrelationswhy
0 thm, holds_from, proposition, opposes, clash, explodes, parallel, law, angle_sum, disagree, similar_possible, size_shows, knot_state, reductio_needed the entire deduction layer, one monotone fixpoint
1 only_in not axiom[H](A) — the only negation in the model
1 forged, leak, malformed, unmoded, undefined_premise boot.rofl's audits, which all read not something

That is a fact about the domain and not about the encoding: the mathematics needs no negation. Deriving theorems from axioms is monotone — more axioms, more theorems, never fewer. The only thing here that needs to say "not" is the meta-question of what one book has that another lacks. Every claim on this page except that table therefore comes out of a single monotone fixpoint, which is also why the semiring folds below are exact: provenance semantics coincide with the Boolean least fixpoint on a stratum.

Step 4 — the rules fire until nothing new appears

4. The fixpoint: which rules fire, and on what

The engine keeps applying rules to facts until applying them again produces nothing new. Three rules do almost all of the work:

thm[G](P) :- axiom[G](P).                                        -- an axiom is a theorem

holds_from[G](S, K1) :- foundation[G](_, _), need_count(S, N), K1 is N + 1.
holds_from[G](S, K)  :- needs(S, K, P), thm[G](P), K1 is K + 1, holds_from[G](S, K1).

thm[G](P) :- step(S, P), holds_from[G](S, 1).                    -- a step whose premises all hold

The middle pair is a counting trick. "All of this step's requirements are satisfied" is a statement about a list, and the direct way of writing it makes the program chase its own tail. So the rules walk the list one position at a time instead, which the engine can do safely.

"Every premise of S holds in G" is a universal over an open set, and the obvious spelling (blocked(S) :- needs(S,_,P), not thm[G](P)) puts thm and blocked in a negative cycle, which the kernel rejects as unstratifiable — correctly. Indexing the premises turns the universal into a positive recursion: holds_from(S, K) means "premises K..N of S all hold", the base case is the position past the last one, and no negation is left. The same device carries examples/moot's flag requirements, and it is a real cost of relation-level stratification, paid deliberately.

Note what the ledger variable [G] does to the search: the rule is one rule, and it fires once per (book, step) pair for which the premises hold. Nine books over 24 steps, with the premise walk in between, is the bulk of the 5351 facts.

Step 5 — the proof tree, which was kept the whole time

5. why: the recorded support, rendered

Ask "why is the angle sum 180 in Euclid's book?" and the answer is the whole proof, from the result down to the starting rules. Read it from the bottom: it needs the exterior-angle result, which needs three of the four uncontroversial postulates, and then the fifth postulate at the top.

Every firing is recorded as a hyperedge from its premises to its conclusion; why walks that record. Nothing is re-derived and nothing is searched — the tree below is the store's support relation, printed. Note [axiom] on the leaves (base facts, no support) and [builtin] on the arithmetic:

Four rule ids appear in the whole tree — rac159495 (axiom→theorem), r1cadcdda and r1c420810 (the premise walk and its base case) and r9749238b (step→theorem) — because the deduction layer is four clauses and that is all of them. Step 6 — the same run, asked different questions

6. The semiring folds: four questions, one support graph

Here is the part that is genuinely unusual. The program worked out what is true once. Everything else — how many proofs there are, which starting rules a result depends on, which proof is shortest — is computed afterwards by walking the same record of what happened, with different arithmetic. The reasoning is not repeated.

The Boolean fixpoint is untouched. A semiring fold replays the recorded support with a different ⊕ and ⊗, so the set of derived facts never changes and only the annotation does:

semiring⊕ / ⊗answers, here
booleanor / andis this theorem derivable in this book
counting+ / ×, with starhow many parallels; how many proofs a theorem has
provenanceunion / pairwise unionwhich axioms carry the theorem
tropicalmin / +the cheapest proof, in rule firings

Each instance declares its convergence discipline, and the fold reports whether the declaration held on this data. Counting is CLOSED — it supplies star, so a cycle answers INFINITE instead of growing for ever; over this store it converges in 21 rounds with 188 facts on cycles. Provenance is BOUNDED, because antichains over a finite base set have finite height.

Step 7 — how a person (or another program) digs in

7. Interrogation: what an agent actually asks

Nothing here is a fixed report. Every line on this page is a question you can ask a different way:

The store is a queryable object, and the interesting workflow is a conversation with it. A typical one, in the order it actually goes:

? thm[G](pythagoras)              -- which books prove it at all
     G = brouwer, dacosta, euclid, frege, schlafli, solid

? thm[G](aaa_congruence)          -- and its rival: no similar triangles
     G = dacosta, frege, lobachevsky, riemann

why thm[euclid](angle_sum_180)    -- the proof, as recorded, not re-derived
whynot thm[saccheri](angle_sum_180)
                                  -- the failure, with the blocking premise named

excise axiom[euclid](post5_unique)
                                  -- delete the axiom, re-evaluate a scratch copy,
                                     and diff: the blast radius IS the answer
     removed: axiom[euclid](post5_unique), thm[euclid](post5_unique),
              thm[euclid](angle_sum_180), thm[euclid](pythagoras),
              thm[euclid](rectangle_exists), thm[euclid](similar_triangles_exist),
              thm[euclid](thales_semicircle)   ... and their support
     added:   only_in[main](brouwer, euclid, post5_unique)   ... and four more

The last one is the best trick: remove the fifth rule from Euclid's book and ask what collapses. Exactly five results fall — and five other books suddenly "have something Euclid does not", which the program also notices on its own.

excise re-evaluates a scratch copy on EDB minus that fact and returns the diff, so the blast radius is measured rather than predicted. The five removed theorems are exactly the set the provenance polynomial marks as needing post5_unique — two independent computations, and the test asserts they agree.

numbers out of axioms

Why big triangles are different from small ones

The angle sum as a function of area, derived twice

In school geometry every triangle's angles add up to 180, whatever its size. In the curved geometries they do not — and the amount they are off by is exactly the triangle's area. So a triangle knows how big it is, and two triangles of the same shape but different size cannot exist. That is not something anybody typed in. Here is where the number comes from:

law[G](K) is the defect coefficient, and it is derived from which angle-sum theorem the book proved. The second rule below uses no law at all: it is the additivity of the defect written in degrees, and it is a neutral theorem.

law[G](0)  :- thm[G](angle_sum_180).
law[G](1)  :- thm[G](defect_proportional_to_area).
law[G](-1) :- thm[G](excess_proportional_to_area).

angle_sum[G](T, S) :- leaf(T, A), law[G](K), S is 180 - K * A.
angle_sum[G](T, S) :- cut(T, T1, T2), angle_sum[G](T1, S1), angle_sum[G](T2, S2),
                      S is S1 + S2 - 180.

The second rule says: cut a triangle in two, and the two halves' angle sums add up to the whole's, plus a straight line's worth of extra. That is true in every geometry. So every big triangle gets its answer worked out twice, by two routes that share nothing — and they agree. If they ever disagreed, the program would say so instead of picking one.

Every compound triangle therefore has its sum derived once from its own area and once per way of composing it out of the cut tree, by routes sharing no rule. angle_sum[euclid](t_whole,180) has 3 derivations under the counting semiring, and they agree.

   lobachevsky       1         178        177        175        173        168
   schlafli          0         180        180        180        180        180
   riemann          -1         182        183        185        187        192
   frege         -1,0,1    3 values   3 values   9 values   3 values  23 values
   brouwer           0         180        180        180        180        180
   dacosta         0,1     178/180    177/180   4 values    173/180   8 values

The empty row is the most honest one on this page. Saccheri's book has no fifth rule at all, in either direction — so it simply does not settle what a triangle's angles add up to. The program answers "nothing" rather than guessing 180 because that is what we are used to.

saccheri has no law, and therefore no angle sum at all. Neutral geometry does not determine it. An engine that answered 180 there would be wrong in a way no test with a fixed expected value would catch — the absence is derived, from the absence of any of the three angle-sum theorems.

And the check that this is a real test and not a rubber stamp: the rule that complains about disagreement does complain, in the two books that hold two contradictory laws at once.

A gate that never fires is an assumption wearing a gate's interface, so disagree/3 is exercised rather than assumed:

    ? disagree[euclid](T, S1, S2)     -> 0 rows
    ? disagree[lobachevsky](T, S1, S2)-> 0 rows
    ? disagree[riemann](T, S1, S2)    -> 0 rows
    ? disagree[dacosta](T, S1, S2)    -> 37 rows
    ? disagree[frege](T, S1, S2)      -> 298 rows

Finally, similarity. In Euclid's book the small triangle and the big one can have the same angles. In the curved books they cannot, and the program says by how much they differ:

    euclid        similar pairs: 1   the two triangles can share their angles
    lobachevsky   similar pairs: 0   t_left (area 5) and t_whole (area 12) differ by 7 degrees
    riemann       similar pairs: 0   t_left (area 5) and t_whole (area 12) differ by 7 degrees

The same statement is also proved symbolically as aaa_congruence — through the proof corpus rather than through arithmetic — in exactly the books where the arithmetic says it. Two independent routes to "a triangle knows its size".

the second axis

Changing the logic instead of the geometry

Constructive and paraconsistent foundations, same rule set

There is a second thing you can change: not what geometry assumes, but what counts as a valid argument.

A logical principle is an axiom like any other here, and a step that argues by contradiction names it among its premises. Nothing in the rules knows what logic is.

step(s_sacc_leg, angle_sum_at_most_180).
needs(s_sacc_leg, 1, exterior_angle).
needs(s_sacc_leg, 2, archimedes).
needs(s_sacc_leg, 3, excluded_middle).      -- the reductio, as a premise

Brouwer's book forbids proving something by showing its opposite is impossible. Take that one line away and exactly two results disappear — and they are exactly the two whose only known proof works that way. The geometry is untouched: Pythagoras still holds.

Removing excluded_middle loses exactly all_or_none and angle_sum_at_most_180, which are exactly the two steps the corpus marks by_reductio. whynot walks the premise chain and stops on thm[brouwer](excluded_middle) — premise 3 of Saccheri–Legendre, named.

Frege's and da Costa's books both contain a flat contradiction: they claim there is exactly one parallel and that there are many. Frege's book also has the traditional rule "from a contradiction, anything follows" — so it proves all 37 statements in the vocabulary, including nonsense about four dimensions it knows nothing about. Da Costa's book drops that one rule, and stays contradictory in one spot while remaining sensible everywhere else.

Explosion is written in the model, not built into the engine:

clash[G](A, B) :- thm[G](A), thm[G](B), opposes[main](A, B).
explodes[G](P) :- clash[G](_, _), axiom[G](ex_falso), proposition[main](P).
thm[G](P)      :- explodes[G](P).
    frege     ex_falso: true   clashes:  28   explodes:  37   theorems: 37 of 37 propositions
    dacosta   ex_falso: false  clashes:   8   explodes:   0   theorems: 28 of 37 propositions

The engine has no ex falso rule of its own, so this is not "ROFL happens not to explode" — it is a paraconsistent foundation and a classical one run through the same rules, differing by one axiom. What da Costa's book does with its contradiction is hold four angle sums at once for the same triangle — 175, 177, 178, 180 degrees — and say so through disagree, instead of picking a winner or refusing to answer. Frege's Grundgesetze is the honest name for the other book: it looked consistent until Russell's letter of 1902.

And the third axis, in one line: dimension

    euclid     dim_plane   -> trefoil unknottable
    solid      dim_space   -> trefoil knotted
    schlafli   dim_four    -> trefoil unties

A knotted piece of string cannot be untied without cutting it — in three dimensions. In four, every knot falls apart. "Obvious" turns out to be a property of the world you grew up in, not of the reasoning.

solid and schlafli differ by that one axiom and nothing else (Wu 1958, Zeeman 1960: an embedded circle in codimension three is isotopic to the unknot). The spec is right that this axis is weaker than the geometry one — a parameter changes, not the logic — which is why it gets one fact and not a section.

the check

How do we know the program is not just confidently wrong?

A complete oracle, and the one gap it found

Everything above is done a second time, in a completely separate program written the boring way, with no rules and no engine — and the two are compared on every single answer. Because each book has only nine or ten starting rules, the slow way can try every possible combination of them, so this is not spot-checking: it is a complete second opinion.

A forward closure over the same corpus in plain TypeScript, sharing no code with the engine beyond reading the same base facts. Then every subset of every book's axioms is closed as well — 29 and 210 of them — which makes the provenance question decided rather than sampled: it is exactly "which axioms can this theorem be proved from, and which can it not be proved without".

    derivability decisions:        333   disagreements: 0
    axiom-necessity decisions:    1942   disagreements: 14  (14 of them conservative)
    axiom-sufficiency checks:      282   disagreements: 0
    minimal axiom sets, found / existing: 236 / 242
    angle-sum comparisons:          45   disagreements: 0
    parallel-witness decisions:     36   disagreements: 0

The one thing that did not match, and why it is reported instead of hidden. For six results in the self-contradicting book, the program's list of "which starting rules did this need" is missing an entry. The reason is a memory limit: that book has so many short proofs (everything follows from its contradiction) that the long honest proof gets crowded out of the list. The effect only ever goes one way — the program says a rule is needed when a proof avoiding it exists, never the reverse — so it errs on the cautious side, and the test pins both the number and the direction so it cannot quietly grow.

Sound everywhere, incomplete in one place, and the size is measured. Every axiom set the polynomial names really does prove the theorem — 282 for 282. It is incomplete for 6 theorems, all in frege, where 6 minimal axiom sets exist that no monomial names. provenanceSemiring keeps at most PROVENANCE_MAX_TERMS = 32 monomials and applies that cap before pruning supersets, so a short proof from a contradiction crowds out the long honest one. The reading is one-directional and it is the safe direction: the polynomial then over-claims necessity, never under-claims it. All 14 necessity disagreements are of that kind, and test/example-goof.test.ts asserts both the count and the direction.

The gap was found by the oracle, not by inspection — and so was a bug in the first version of the projection itself, which collected axioms from any book because a derivation by explosion passes through proposition[main](P). The oracle reported it as a minimality failure, and it was one: the projection was wrong, not the polynomial.

the cost

The one thing this example could not do cleanly, until it was fixed

Where the kernel fought back, and what it took to fix

The whole point here is that the reasoning is written once and works for every book. But the safety check that watches for one book secretly reading another could not tell the difference between "this rule works on whichever book you point it at" and "this rule reads one book and writes into another", so it raised a false alarm, once. Nobody switched the check off. The check was taught to tell the two apart, and this example is what paid for it.

Every rule's reflection records reads_from and writes_to per perspective, and a variable perspective used to reduce to $any at both ends. boot.rofl then saw a flow from $any into $any, could not see the two were the same variable, and reported a leak. There was no way to declare that bridge either: bridge_decl is kernel-emitted only when the head perspective differs from the premise's, and $any is not writable in the surface syntax.

examples/sus and examples/aka met the same limit and could write one rule per ledger instead. GOOF cannot — one rule per ledger is the thesis failing — and that is what made the limit worth repairing rather than working around. src/reflect.ts now records a variable perspective as itself, $var("G"), so a rule polymorphic in the ledger carries one term at both ends, and boot.rofl reads a flow whose ends are one term as the identity it is while still reporting a rule that reads ?G and writes somewhere undeclared. The false row is gone; the planted one still bites:

      leak[audit](euclid, main)        -- a REAL leak, planted, and caught

The demo still expands the rule set one copy per book, because the comparison is the thesis; what it measures now is only the rule count:

    polymorphic: 1716 facts     expanded: 1716 facts     IDENTICAL
    leak[audit] rows — polymorphic: 0   expanded: 0
    28 clauses become 292 — and one of them, the rule that compares two books,
    becomes 81, because it ranges over PAIRS of ledgers.

Two smaller scars, both still visible in the file's shape. A head written without brackets defaults to [main] without the explicit flag, so no bridge_decl is emitted and the leak audit fires on a rule doing nothing wrong — which is why proposition[main](P) :- axiom[G](P). spells out a perspective that looks redundant. And explodes/1 exists as its own relation purely so that whynot renders "the book does not explode" as one line instead of enumerating every pair of theorems that failed to contradict each other. The shape of the demonstration is part of the model's design.

examples/goof/ in the ROFL repository. Everything quoted on this page is demo.ts's stdout, unedited, and test/example-goof.test.ts pins it. Run it: node --experimental-strip-types examples/goof/demo.ts

The proof corpus is a hand-built abstraction of classical proofs, not a formalisation of them: each step is one fact saying "this follows from these", and nothing here checks that it does. What is computed exactly is everything downstream. No novelty is claimed — intuitionistic Datalog, paraconsistent logics and reverse mathematics are old fields; what is shown is that a general-purpose rule engine with ledgers and semirings answers their questions without knowing any geometry.
