NOPEaccess control, explained
A worked example on the ROFL kernel

Which no stopped you, and how many ways in are left

Some rules say yes. Some rules say no. No always wins. When a computer tells you "access denied", it almost never tells you which no — and it never tells you the more useful thing: if you can get in, how many different ways could you still get in if someone took one of them away?

Deny-overrides-Allow is not a priority pass bolted onto a policy engine. It is stratified negation, which a Datalog kernel does natively: one negated premise, an evaluation order derived from the rule dependency graph rather than written down. And once the decision is a fixpoint over a support hypergraph, the same fact answers questions a Boolean simulator cannot phrase — starting with how many independent derivations grant this access.

6 independent ways bob can write to the production bucket Eight policy grants reach the request; two are cut by a permission boundary. Revoke any single one of the survivors and he still has access.
1 · No always wins

Four places a rule can live. A no in any of them ends it.

Permissions are not one list. They are four lists stacked on top of each other, written by four different groups of people who mostly do not talk to each other: the company, the security team, whoever set up your account, and whoever owns the thing you are trying to touch.

Every list can say yes and every list can say no. The rule is simple and it is the same everywhere: if any list says no, the answer is no, no matter how many other lists said yes.

The model is AWS-shaped: a service control policy at the organizational unit, a permission boundary on the identity, identity policies attached to users, groups and roles, and a resource policy on the bucket. Each level contributes allow and deny statements, gated by conditions over principal tags.

The permission boundary is the interesting one: it is not an explicit Deny but the absence of an Allow, so it is modelled as a negation over what the boundary permits — a second stratum, one level below the Deny-overrides rule.

1Service control policy — the organization Deny s3:* on prod-bucket/* when aws:PrincipalTag/env != prod. This is the one that stopped alice.
2Permission boundary — the identity ci_runner may only write to artifacts/*. Not a Deny: a missing Allow.
3Identity policy — user, group, role data_reader allows s3:GetObject on prod-bucket/*. The yes that everyone looks at first.
4Resource policy — the bucket Allows bob to write; denies s3:DeleteObject to everyone, including the role holding Allow * on *.

Alice asked to read one file. A rule on her role said yes. A rule three floors up, written by the company and not by her team, said no — because a label on her account says staging and the rule wants prod. Every tool she has will tell her "denied". This one tells her which floor, which rule, and which label.

Asked directly, whynot walks the rule that could have concluded the fact, names the premise that failed, and then explains that premise, down to a builtin failing on real values. Here it bottoms out on the negated premise of the Deny-overrides rule, naming the witness that blocks it; why on that witness is the level / policy / condition answer.

2 · Where the precedence comes from

Nobody wrote "no beats yes". It fell out of the order.

You might expect a program full of instructions: collect all the rules, sort them, check the nos before the yeses. That is how these systems are usually built, and it is where the bugs live — someone forgets a level, or checks it in the wrong order.

Here there is one sentence. You get in if some rule lets you in, and nothing blocks you. The word "nothing" is what does all the work: to know that nothing blocks you, the machine has to finish looking for blocks first. So it does. The order is not a decision anyone made — it is forced by the sentence.

Deny-overrides is a stratum, not a priority pass.

A negated premise in a stratified Datalog means "after the layer that concludes it has reached its fixpoint". boot.rofl computes stratum/2 from the rule dependency graph with three ordinary rules, as data; the kernel reads the resulting facts and contains no stratification checker of its own. A model with circular negation is rejected at load with a trace instead of silently picking an order.

route(P, A, R, Route) :- grant(P, Q, A, R, Route), not blocked(P, Q, A, R).

These are the strata from an actual run — the AWS evaluation order, computed rather than written down. route sits strictly above every rule that can conclude a Deny, so every Deny is complete before any Allow survives:

0 statementaction_inresource_inpolicy_ofboundary_allowshas_condviaabsent
1 appliesgrantdeny_atblocked
2 routeaccess

Stratum 1 carries two negations of its own: applies reads not has_cond (a statement with no Condition block applies to everyone) and deny_at reads not boundary_allows (a permission boundary is a missing Allow). Add a fifth policy level tomorrow and the precedence code does not change, because there isn't any.

The machine sorts the rules into layers by itself, and only then runs them: everything that could possibly say no, first; the decision, last.

3 · The number nobody checks

Bob can get in six different ways.

Bob is on a team. The team has a key. He also has his own key, and he is allowed to borrow two robots' keys, and one of those robots can borrow a third robot's key, and that third robot can open everything.

So when someone files a ticket that says "remove bob's access" and takes away the team key, bob still has five ways in. Nobody notices, because the tool they checked with only knows how to say yes or no. This is the real reason permission cleanups in big companies run for years and the risk never goes down.

Privilege sprawl is a count, and the count is a fold.

The Boolean answer and the sprawl number come from the same fact, access(bob, "s3:PutObject", ...), folded with a different semiring over the support the engine already recorded. Nothing is re-derived and the query language is not extended: the store keeps every witness, and evaluateSemiring walks them.

bobp_bucket / s_bucket_bob
bobp_dev_write / s_dev_put
bob→deployerp_deploy / s_put_prod
bob→ci_runner→admin_legacyp_admin / s_admin_all

Eight grants, two cut, six survivors. Note what the boundary on ci_runner does not do: it caps what ci_runner may do, not what ci_runner may become. Both routes passing through ci_runner to admin_legacy survive it.

What the number means here. A count of derivations reads differently per domain, so the sign has to be stated rather than assumed: this one measures how robust the access is to revocation, and robustness of a grant nobody intended is exactly the risk. Six is not six times as much access — it is one access that survives five separate revocations, which makes the count a remediation-cost estimate.

  revocation drill -- take one grant away, ask again:

    revoke member_of(bob, developers)         -> access true, paths 5
    revoke assumes(bob, deployer)             -> access true, paths 4
    revoke attached(admin_legacy, p_admin)    -> access true, paths 4
4 · The cycle

Two robots can borrow each other's keys. That is not infinite ways in.

Two of the robots can each borrow the other's key, round and round forever. If you count "routes" the lazy way, you get infinity — you can always take one more lap. But a lap gets you nothing you did not already have, so a lap is not a way in.

So the count is over paths that never visit the same robot twice. Add another loop to the map and the number does not move.

Simple paths, because a lap is not a derivation of new privilege.

Assume-role chains genuinely cycle, and the number of walks through a cyclic graph is infinite. The fix is not a smaller semiring — it is putting the path inside the fact:

via(P, P, cons(P, nil)) :- identity(P). via(P, Q, cons(Q, C)) :- via(P, X, C), assumes(X, Q), absent(Q, C).

Every derivation extends the chain by one identity not already on it, so the role graph may cycle all it likes while the support hypergraph of via stays acyclic — and a cycle that is not in the support hypergraph cannot inflate a fold over it. Chain membership is positive, not negated: its term domain is generated from chains already derived, so the whole thing is monotone recursion over terms and never argues with the stratification checker.

Asked as routes-in-a-graph

infinitely many

access_naive(bob, s3:PutObject, ...) — no chain in the fact, so the support hypergraph is cyclic, and the counting semiring is CLOSED: star(1) = INFINITE. That is the engine being honest, not a bug. It is the wrong question.

Asked as simple paths

6

access(bob, s3:PutObject, ...) — the chain is in the fact, nothing is on a support cycle, and the fold returns a number. Close a second cycle in the role graph and it stays 6.

And the cycle is a finding by itself. deployer and ci_runner can each assume the other, so compromising either is compromising both — something no review that looks at roles one at a time will see. Detecting it is free: it is the same strongly-connected component the fold already computes to decide where star applies.

Both formulations agree on the verdict on every triple — walk reachability and simple-path reachability coincide, and all 45 access facts match. Only the count differs. The discipline was changed in the model, not by reaching for depthBoundedCountingSemiring(n): that answers "how many derivations of height at most n", which is a different question whose answer moves when you change n, and a metric with a knob on it does not belong in a report.

5 · How we know it is right

We checked every possible question. All 72 of them.

This world is small: six people and robots, four things you can do, three things you can do them to. That is 72 questions in total — so we asked all of them twice. Once through the rule engine, and once with a completely separate program that just walks the policies by hand.

They agreed on all 72, on the answer and on the number of ways in. If they had disagreed, that would be the finding — and the rules would not be quietly edited until the disagreement went away.

For a finite model, exhaustive enumeration is a complete oracle.

Every (principal, action, resource) triple is decided a second time by a direct evaluation of the policy set in plain TypeScript — a DFS over the assume-role graph with a visited set, no engine, no rules, no shared code beyond reading the same base facts — and compared on both the verdict and the route count. Comparing counts, not only verdicts, is what makes it an oracle for the metric rather than just for the decision.

    principals x actions x resources = 6 x 4 x 3 = 72 triples
    verdict disagreements: 0
    route-count disagreements: 0

A second check runs on the model itself: no rule may fall outside range restriction and no relation may be evaluated top-down. That one earned its place — it caught the resource-policy Deny rule leaving the acting identity unbound while this example was being written, which would have made deny_at a demand relation and put its facts outside the fold entirely.

6 · Five questions, one fixpoint

Same fact, different semiring.

Every IAM simulator on the market, AWS's own included, is the first row of this table. The remaining rows are the questions asked at security audits, answered today by hand and incompletely.

The kernel knows "a semiring", never "the tropical semiring" — the instances live outside src/, each declaring the convergence discipline it honours, and the fold reports whether that declaration actually held on this data.
