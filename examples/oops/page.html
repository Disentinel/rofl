A paper was retracted.
What is now in doubt?

A science paper turned out to be wrong. Some other papers trusted it. Which of those are shaky now — and which are fine, because they had other reasons too?

The retraction is recorded. The conclusions that leaned on it keep living. Every tool answers is X retracted; none answers what became unreliable. This one does, in 25 rules over a 46-fact ledger.

Papers stand on other papers

A citation is not a dependency

Scientists build on each other. A new paper leans on older ones the way a floor of a building leans on the floor below.

But there are two very different ways to lean. Sometimes a paper's whole argument rests on an older one — pull the older one out and the newer one falls down. And sometimes a paper just mentions the older one in passing, the way you might say "other people have looked at this too". Pull that one out and nothing happens.

When a paper turns out to be wrong, it gets retracted — the journal officially takes it back. Right now, computers can tell you which papers mentioned it. Nobody can tell you which ones fall down.

The distinction is not who cited the retracted paper. It is whose conclusion depended on it.

A paper that named the retracted work in its introduction is unharmed. A paper whose only argument rests on it is destroyed. Today those are the same row in the same database. So the model carries two kinds of citation edge, and only one of them carries poison:

depends_on(A, B) :- citation(A, B, load_bearing, Yc), clock(Y), Yc <= Y.
mentions(A, B)   :- citation(A, B, mention, Yc),      clock(Y), Yc <= Y.

The picture

The cascade, and where it stops

helix_2019 is the paper that turned out to be wrong. yield_2020 leaned on it for everything, so yield_2020 is shaky. scaleup_2021 leaned only on yield_2020, so it is shaky too — the problem travels.

And then it stops. meta_2022 leans on the shaky scaleup_2021 — but it also leans on thermo_2016, which is perfectly fine. So meta_2022 still has a leg to stand on. It is not shaky. And policy_2023, which stands on meta_2022, is not shaky either.

That is the whole trick. Knowing which papers touched the wrong one is easy and useless — it would paint half the picture orange. Knowing which ones have nothing else to stand on is the answer people actually need.

Two monotone reachability relations and one stratified negation between them:

tainted(P)  :- depends_on(P, B), retracted(B).      -- some chain reaches a wreck
tainted(P)  :- depends_on(P, B), tainted(B).

grounded(P) :- own_evidence(P), not retracted(P).   -- some chain still stands
grounded(P) :- depends_on(P, B), grounded(B), not retracted(P).

at_risk(P)  :- tainted(P), not grounded(P).         -- every chain runs through it
robust(P)   :- tainted(P), grounded(P).             -- touched, and survives it

tainted alone is the useless answer everyone already has: touched by the retraction, which paints half the graph. The stopping condition is the product. A robust paper is not at risk, and a paper that depends only on a robust paper is grounded through it — so the cascade dies there instead of running to the leaves.

The natural formulation is mutually recursive through negation (at_risk unless a pillar is clean; a pillar is clean unless it is at risk) and the kernel rejects it — correctly, since that program has no unique least model. Splitting it into reaches a wreck, reaches something standing, and one negation between them makes it computable and clearer about what is being claimed.

Now watch the years go by

The knowledge state is a function of the tick

Drag the slider. Papers appear when they are published. In 2022 one paper is taken back; in 2024 another one is. In 2025 somebody cites the retracted paper anyway. And in 2026 the retraction is itself taken back — and everything that fell down stands up again, all by itself.

One program, one store, and the year is an input. Every status relation carries clock(Y) in its body, so a paper does not exist before it is published, a citation does not exist before it is made, and retracted is derived from the retraction and un-retraction events against the clock. Nothing below is patched when the clock moves. The facts are simply derived again.

Each year here is one tick. The same sets come out of running the world forward one tick per year and out of evaluating the program once with the clock set to that year — 142 domain facts, identical both ways.

The computer does not remember an answer and fix it up. It works the whole thing out again, from scratch, for whichever year you point at.

How do we know it is right?

The oracle: two computations of the same thing

Here is the nice part. There are two completely different ways to ask "what did this retraction cost us?", and they were both run.

The first way is the rules above: follow the leaning, mark what has nothing left to stand on. The second way is to rub the retraction out entirely — take it out of the pile of facts, work the whole world out again from nothing, and see which answers disappeared. If the two ways ever disagreed, something would be wrong. They agree exactly.

And rubbing out a retraction does not only delete things. It brings papers back: three of them stand on evidence again. That is the same machinery a real un-retraction uses.

excise(F) re-runs the fixpoint on the EDB minus F and returns the diff. That is not a way to compute the blast radius — it is the blast radius, and it shipped before this example existed. It is a clean re-evaluation, so multiple support is handled by construction: a fact with two independent derivations does not fall when one support is removed, and nothing had to be told about that.

So the same question gets answered twice by different machinery — once by the derived attribution at_risk_from(P, R), once by subtracting the retraction from the EDB and re-deriving everything:

excisefacts -/+at_risk removed= at_risk_from

The check is discriminating because the two retracted roots have disjoint cascades: excising the 2024 retraction removes three of the five at-risk facts and leaves the other two standing. If the rules over-propagated, or excise over-removed, the sets would differ. They do not, for either root, at either the at_risk or the larger tainted level.

added is the striking half: excising a retraction adds grounded(yield_2020), because with the retraction gone those papers stand on evidence again. Un-retraction and blast radius turn out to be the same operation read in two directions.

How shaky is shaky?

Counting is the robustness metric

A paper with one reason behind it is fragile. A paper with three separate reasons survives losing one of them. So the machine counts the reasons:

One surviving support chain is fragile; three survive a retraction. Folding the counting semiring over the recorded support gives that number directly — the number of distinct derivations of grounded(P):

preprint_a and preprint_b cite each other. So you can walk in a circle between them forever, and there is no honest number of reasons to give. The machine says infinitely many instead of making something up. Real science does contain little loops like this.

INFINITE is not an overflow: it is a member of the carrier. preprint_a and preprint_b cite each other, so each trip round the loop is another derivation tree, and the counting semiring is CLOSED — it multiplies facts on a cycle by star(one) and says so. Real citation graphs contain cycles; a robustness score that silently invented a finite number there would be worse than one that refuses.

Where the kernel fought back

Three places worth stating plainly, because they are the honest cost of doing time properly. Persistence is not a storage property here: the ledger survives a tick only because six @next carry rules move it forward. That means a carried fact's support names the same fact one tick earlier — a self-loop — and past tick 0 the counting semiring used to report infinitely many for everything, including a paper that cites nothing. This example is what measured that, and it is settled: the fold is about one tick, so a fact that arrived over the boundary is a given in the tick that reads it, with count one. The ticked store and the store evaluated at a clock value now agree on all 142 domain facts and on every count over them; the cycle that stays infinitely many is the two preprints citing each other, inside a single tick.

For the same reason a why tree past tick 0 carries [cycle] and [past tick] leaves — that is what persistence-by-rule looks like in provenance, rendered honestly rather than hidden. And "the state at an earlier tick" is queryable as provenance, not as facts: tick-scoped facts are dropped at the boundary, but frozen derived_by/3 survives, so the tick-5 store still reports the four at_risk facts it derived at tick 4 beside the five it holds now.

What is real here and what is not. The 19 papers and 23 citation edges are synthetic and hand-labelled, small enough to check every verdict by hand. Everything computed from them — the sets, the counts, the diffs, the timeline — is real output from a real run. On live data the retraction facts (Retraction Watch, Crossref) and the citation graph (OpenAlex) are hard ground truth; the citation type is the one field open data labels badly. Two honest options: run on a labelled subset, or treat every citation as load-bearing and publish the result as an upper bound, clearly labelled. An upper bound is still strictly more than "is X retracted?", and it tightens as labels improve without anything else changing.
