SUSsuspicion, counted
A worked example on the ROFL kernel

Everybody says things. Some of them are lying.

Eight players, two of them secretly traitors. Nobody can see who. What people can do is talk — and some of what they say is false on purpose. This tool works out every way the two traitors could have been chosen so that everyone honest is still telling the truth all at once, and then counts how many of those ways make you the culprit.

A perspective here is not a technical detail, it is the subject. Each player writes into their own ledger; two contradicting claims coexist because their perspectives differ; who wrote a fact is the load identity checked against authority, so an entry in somebody else's book is forged[audit] with no enforcement code. On top of that, the suspicion metric is model counting — one fold of a counting semiring over the support the Boolean fixpoint already recorded.

5 / 5 green is a traitor in every world that still holds together — in round 3 Twenty-three of the twenty-eight ways to deal the two traitor cards are already ruled out by something somebody said. Green is in all five that are left. Green is innocent.
1 · Two people, one round, two stories

Both statements are kept. Neither is thrown away.

Pink says green was in the engine room when the murder happened. Green says green was in the canteen. They cannot both be right — nobody is in two places at once.

A normal program would have to pick one, or refuse to run. This one keeps both, because it does not store what happened. It stores who said what, and those two things never contradict each other. What it works out from the pair is the useful bit: at least one of pink and green is a traitor.

The contradiction is a derived fact, not a load error.

The two claims are different facts because their perspective differs, so the program stays loadable and the fixpoint stays total. No rule anywhere says "X unless not-X"; the incompatibility is derived and named.

pink's book
said[pink](k5, saw(green, electrical), 2, 2).

"I saw green by the engine room in round 2."

Loaded under who=pink; authority(pink, pink) is the entire list of who may write here.

green's book
said[green](k7, saw(green, cafeteria), 2, 2).

"No — I was in the canteen the whole round."

The accused's own testimony is a claim like any other, and structurally the weakest kind: it contradicts everybody.

Both hold. Nothing was ranked, resolved or dropped. What the engine derives from the pair is the finding: clash(k5, k7) — at most one of pink and green is crew.
clash[case](K1, K2) :- sighting[case](K1, _, Q, R1, T), sighting[case](K2, _, Q, R2, T), R1 != R2, claim_no(K1, N1), claim_no(K2, N2), N1 < N2.

And the forgery check needs no rule at all. Load one entry into green's ledger under who=pink and forged[audit] returns exactly that fact — because asserted_by is the load identity, checked against authority. The same forgery in a single ledger with a claimed_by column is a perfectly well-formed row, and no audit in any kernel can see it.

There is also no way to write in somebody else's notebook. If pink tries to add a line to green's book, the tool notices immediately — not because anyone wrote a rule against forgery, but because every book has a list of who is allowed to write in it, and pink is not on green's.

2 · The number

There are 28 ways to pick two traitors out of eight. Cross out the impossible ones.

Start with every possible pairing — red and blue, red and green, and so on: twenty-eight in total. Then go through everything anybody said, and cross out any pairing that would need an honest person to have lied.

Whatever is left is what could still be true. If you appear in most of what is left, you look guilty. If you appear in none of it, you are in the clear. That is the whole idea, and it is exactly what people at the table do in their heads — only badly, because nobody can hold twenty-eight stories at once.

The suspicion is a model count, and it comes out of the fold.

One bridging assumption carries the model — crew never lie, traitors may — and every consistency rule is a consequence of it. A world is refuted when some live claim, read under that assumption, is false in it; the refutation names its own reason, so it is attributable rather than a bare Boolean.

outcome[case](guilty, P) :- guilty_in[worlds](P, W). outcome[case](any, P) :- consistent[worlds](W), player[public](P, _).

outcome(guilty, P) has exactly one derivation per consistent world in which P is a traitor, and outcome(any, P) one per consistent world. The counting semiring folded over the recorded support returns both numbers, and their ratio is the suspicion. Nothing is enumerated in TypeScript to get it; the query language is not extended.

3 · Accuse in round 3, withdraw in round 5

The tool was confidently, correctly, completely wrong.

By round 3 there is no story left in which green is innocent, so the table votes green out. Two rounds later pink's card is turned over and pink is a traitor — which means two of the things said about green were lies, and half the stories that were crossed out should never have been.

The interesting part is not that it changed its mind. It is that it can say exactly which sentence, said by whom, in which round stopped holding — and that the decision the table already acted on is marked, not quietly rewritten.

A standard of proof is a policy, so it lives outside the rules.

The count is a measurement; the threshold on it is not. Three consistent worlds in four, declared in demo.ts beside clearsThreshold, which makes the same separation for the Viterbi carrier. The rules never mention it.

Round 4 is worth a look: red admits a mistake and takes back what red said. That single retraction puts a story back on the table — and it is the true one, pink and cyan.

The two annulments are not the same arithmetic, and that is this example's unexpected finding. A withdrawal restores worlds: red may be crew, so red's word was load-bearing wherever red is crew. An unmasking changes no count at all — in every world where pink is a traitor, pink's claims never constrained anything to begin with, so the model had already priced the possibility that pink was lying. The annulled / at_risk machinery therefore explains the round-5 change to a person; it does not compute it. Model counting does that on its own, silently, and would never tell the table that the vote it already took no longer stands.

Which link broke

The case against green was never one thing. Three different people said something that put green in the frame, and the tool keeps their three cases apart on purpose — so that when one of them falls over, it can name it.

Each accuser's case is its own conclusion in the support graph, so the marking is per-accuser rather than per-target. The graph is built on clash and stated, never on conflict and live: annulling a claim must not quietly erase the fact that the case ever rested on it.

collapsedcase_against(green, pink) k5, k9 — both annulled in round 5 when pink's card was shown. This is the accusation the table voted on.
collapsedcase_against(green, red) k10 — withdrawn in round 4. Red admitted the sighting was a mistake.
standscase_against(green, cyan) k6 — cyan also placed green at the scene, and nobody has asked cyan anything. It is the only thing still keeping green in the frame.
shakenejection(green) The decision the table took in round 3. Marked transitively, through finding(green), because part of what it rested on is gone and part is not.
rests_on[case](case_against(Q, P), K) :- points_at[case](Q, K), claimant[case](K, P). rests_on[case](finding(Q), case_against(Q, P)) :- case_against[case](Q, P). rests_on[case](ejection(Q), finding(Q)) :- ejected[public](Q, _), case_against[case](Q, _). rests_on[case](X, Z) :- rests_on[case](X, Y), rests_on[case](Y, Z).
4 · The price of an accusation

"No" is not an answer. The answer is what you would have to call a lie.

When the tool says green cannot be the traitor, it does not stop there. It lists every story that would make green guilty, and for each one, the exact statements standing in the way. Want to accuse green anyway? Fine — here is the bill, itemised.

Players do this in their heads all the time, and get it wrong. "You'd have to think lime is lying and that the card was faked" is a real argument, and it is usually made badly because nobody actually works out the list.

One relation, read from both ends.

price[case](P, W, R) :- world[worlds](W), traitor_in[worlds](W, P), refuted_by[worlds](W, R). incriminates[case](P, W, R) :- world[worlds](W), crew_in[worlds](W, P), refuted_by[worlds](W, R).

price is simultaneously what clears P and what you must retract to accuse P. Same set, opposite reading. The cheapest blocker set over the guilty worlds is the price.

The kernel's own whynot bottoms out on the same blocked premise, one negated literal down: not impossible[worlds](w(green,lime)) -- blocked: impossible[worlds](w(green,lime)) holds. The rendering above is the same information arranged for a person at a table.

5 · Time, and the trap in it

"What did we know in round 3?" is a question you can ask.

Rounds are time. Something said in round 6 must not be allowed to change what the table could reasonably have believed in round 3, and something revealed in round 5 has to be able to undo a conclusion from round 3.

There are two ways to get back to round 3: play the game forward again round by round, or just set the clock to 3 and ask. Both give the same answer here — checked, at every round, fact for fact.

The rule this example taught, and its retirement.

The rule was fold as-of, never over a ticked store. A fact @next :- fact carry rule makes every carried fact its own support one tick back; past tick 0 the CLOSED counting semiring therefore answered "infinitely many" for everything — while every Boolean answer stayed correct. Silent, total, and fatal to an example whose whole product is a count. It was retired on 2026-08-30 by a decision rather than a workaround: not p means "not derivable in the CURRENT tick's store", and by the same argument the fold is about one tick, so a fact that arrived over the boundary is a given in the tick that reads it. Measured here, since this is where the as-of machinery was built: at all six rounds the two stores agree on every domain fact and on every count, and at round 3 tropical, provenance and Viterbi agree too.

Ticked and as-of agree on the domain facts at every round: 1226, 1010, 1484, 1412, 1209, 1503 facts, list for list.

Same count

5 = 5

outcome(any, green) is 5 as-of and 5 after the ticks. It used to be uncountable after two ticks; 20 facts lie on a support cycle either way.

What a past tick does still answer is the frozen derived_by record — 88 refuted_by facts were derived at tick 2 and are still on the books. What it does not answer is what could have been asked at tick 2: tick-scoped facts are dropped at the boundary. asOf(3) answers that second question, and it is a different store rather than a view of this one. Stated here rather than papered over, because the difference is easy to miss and easy to claim.

6 · How we know it is right

We checked all 168 answers by hand. Well — by a second program.

Twenty-eight possible pairs of traitors, six rounds: 168 yes-or-no questions in total. Each one was answered twice — once by the rule engine, and once by a completely separate program that just walks through what everybody said and checks it. They agreed on all 168, and on every count.

For a finite model, enumeration is a complete oracle.

No engine, no rules, no shared code beyond reading the same base facts, and compared on the verdict and on the counting semiring's guilty/total numbers — comparing counts is what makes it an oracle for the metric rather than only for the decision. A disagreement would be the finding; the model is never tuned until one goes away.

    verdict disagreements:     0
    guilty-count disagreements: 0
    consistent worlds by round: r1=22  r2=9  r3=5  r4=5  r5=2  r6=1

A second check runs on the model itself and is asserted in the test rather than printed and forgotten: no rule may fall outside range restriction, and no relation may be evaluated top-down. It earned its place — it caught the alibi rule leaving the kill room unbound while this example was being written, which would have made elsewhere a demand relation and put its facts outside the fold entirely.

7 · Five questions, one fixpoint

Same facts, different semiring.

SemiringQuestionAnswer here
BooleanIs this role assignment possible at all?5 of 28, in round 3
countingIn how many of them is green a traitor?5 — the suspicion metric
ViterbiWhich possible world is most likely, given who lies boldly?w(pink,cyan), 65.8% of the mass at round 5
provenanceWhich claims produce this refutation?one minimal source set per refutation
tropicalWhat is the shortest chain to the conclusion?5 rule firings

The Viterbi arm never changes which worlds are possible: it is a prior on people laid over an answer the Boolean fixpoint already fixed, and the test asserts the ranked set is exactly the consistent set. Its independence assumption is stated in the model rather than hidden — the score is a product over claims, so it treats each claim's honesty as independent given the roles, which is false of any table where liars coordinate.

Run it. node --experimental-strip-types examples/sus/demo.ts — zero dependencies, Node 22.6+ or Bun. The model is examples/sus/sus.rofl; the properties above are tested in test/example-sus.test.ts.

Data is synthetic and hand-built: the scenario is arranged so that the engine crosses a standard of proof and then falls back under it, which is a thing you construct rather than a thing you find.
