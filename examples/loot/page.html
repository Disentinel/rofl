He picks a book off a corpse
and reads it.

The book is full of rules. Once he has read it, the rules are in his head and he behaves differently. Some books make him cleverer. Some books get him killed.

A book is a rulepack. In ROFL rules are facts, so reading one is load and a copy of the store is a copy of the rule set. Two readers walk one road: one installs each book in a fork and diffs before absorbing it, one swallows.

What is in a book

What a rulepack is

Our wanderer already knows a few things. If a beast is armed and moving, it is dangerous. If a chest has no lure-mark on it, it is safe to open. That is his whole head: fourteen little rules.

A book adds more rules. A glowing thing in wet ground will burn you. Read that, and now he knows something he did not know before — which is good, because there is a glowing thing in the bog ahead.

But a book is not just its rules. A rule talks about things like glowing and wet, and somebody has to actually be able to look at the world and say whether a thing is glowing. If the book asks about something our wanderer cannot see — heat, say, when he has no way to feel heat — then the book will sit in his head saying nothing at all. And saying nothing looks exactly like saying "all clear".

So every book comes with a list on its first page: here are my rules, here is what I need you to be able to see, and here is which version of the world I was written for. Without that list, he does not read it.

Not rules. Rules, plus the predicate vocabulary they read, plus the version of the extractor that produces it.

A rule is not self-standing: it names predicates, and predicates come out of whatever turns the world into facts. A manifest is therefore five relations, living in the book's own ledger and signed by its author — and the rule list is content hashes, computed by the packer from the very text that ships beside it:

pack[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns).
pack_title[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns, "A Codex of Thorns").
pack_author[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns, thornwood_of_the_low_fen).
pack_extractor[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns, 2).
pack_needs[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns, glows).
pack_rule[codex_of_thorns](codex_of_thorns, ra83754c7).

ruleIdOf(c) is r + fnv1a of the canonical clause, so two rules are the same rule iff they are literally the same clause. Nobody has to be trusted for the tamper check to work: the hash is the name, so a text that does not hash to the name it shipped under is not that rule.

Three ways a book is unusable before one rule of it runs, and they want different remedies:

incomplete[audit](P)  :- known_pack[audit](P), not pack_extractor[P](P, _).
version_gap[audit](P, V, W) :- pack_extractor[P](P, V), extractor_version[world](W), V != W.
missing_predicate[audit](P, Rel) :- pack_needs[P](P, Rel), not produces[world](Rel).
mute[audit](P) :- missing_predicate[audit](P, _).

pack[P](P) reads through a variable perspective — "in whichever book's ledger it lives". A pack's id and the name of its ledger are the same atom on purpose.

Flipping through first

Quarantine: the fork, the diff, the ruling

Here is the whole trick, and it is simpler than it sounds.

Before he absorbs a book, the careful wanderer makes a copy of his own head. He reads the book into the copy, and then he compares: what does the copy believe that I do not? And — this is the important half — what did I believe that the copy no longer does?

If the copy only ever knows more, the book is safe and he takes it, no thinking required. If reading the book made him forget something, he leaves it on the corpse.

Then he throws the copy away. Nothing that happened in the copy ever happened to him.

Rules are facts, so a fork of the store is a fork of the rule set. Quarantine is Rofl.fromSnapshot(r.save()) and no new machinery at all.

The verdict is a rule, not a branch in TypeScript:

gives[audit](P)         :- gained[quarantine](P, _, _).
takes_away[audit](P)    :- lost[quarantine](P, _, _).
trespass[audit](P, Rel) :- gained[quarantine](P, Rel, _), guarded[main](Rel).
overrules[audit](P, T)  :- gained[quarantine](P, safe, T), suspect[mind](T).

accept[audit](P) :- proposed[quarantine](P), gives[audit](P),
                    not takes_away[audit](P), not trespass[audit](P, _),
                    not overrules[audit](P, _).
refuse[audit](P) :- proposed[quarantine](P), takes_away[audit](P).

So the ruling can be interrogated like anything else:

$ why refuse[audit](grimoire_of_ash)
refuse[audit](grimoire_of_ash)  <= rcb77ef2f @tick 0
  proposed[quarantine](grimoire_of_ash) [axiom]
  takes_away[audit](grimoire_of_ash)  <= rbec1c811 @tick 0
    lost[quarantine](grimoire_of_ash,opens,supply_chest) [axiom]

Conservativity is not a separate ritual. accept is "it took nothing away". The two extra clauses of refuse — a book concluding into a relation the reader keeps to itself, and a book reversing a verdict already held — sit on top of conservativity rather than weakening it.

How a new rule can make you forget

The one door a foreign rule has into your existing beliefs

This is the part that surprises people. Adding a rule should only ever teach you more — how can learning something make you forget?

Because some of what he knows is shaped like "unless". He thinks a chest is safe to open unless something about it looks suspicious. The Grimoire of Ash adds one new reason to be suspicious: scorch marks. And the supply chest in the corpse field survived a fire.

So the new rule does not contradict him. It just fills in a hole in an "unless", and a thing he could do a minute ago he can no longer do. Nothing warned him. The chest simply stopped being openable.

Datalog is monotone in facts and not in rules. Negation is the door, and the native head has exactly one negated head-relation.

suspect[mind](C) :- container[world](C), lure_sign[world](C).
safe[mind](C)    :- container[world](C), not suspect[mind](C).
opens[mind](C)   :- container[world](C), safe[mind](C), at[world](R), here[world](C, R).

-- and the Grimoire of Ash, one rule:
suspect[mind](C) :- container[world](C), scorched[world](C).

Adding a rule cannot add a safe fact; it can only take one away. Nothing in the native head anticipates the grimoire — the door is structural, and it is the only one.

The other direction reads just as well. With four books swallowed, whynot names both rules that could have opened the chest and why each failed — one native, blocked by a foreign premise; one foreign, failing on its own terms:

whynot opens[mind](supply_chest):
  rule r8f5f8191: opens[mind](?C)@now :- container[world](?C)@now, safe[mind](?C)@now, at[world](?R)@now, here[world](?C,?R)@now
    failed premise: safe[mind](supply_chest)
      rule r080165dd: safe[mind](?C)@now :- container[world](?C)@now, not suspect[mind](?C)@now
        failed premise: not suspect[mind](supply_chest) -- blocked: suspect[mind](supply_chest) holds
      rule r5dcbb40a: safe[mind](?C)@now :- container[world](?C)@now, ward_glyph[world](?C)@now
        failed premise: ward_glyph[world](supply_chest)
          no rule concludes 'ward_glyph' and no matching base fact exists

The rule ids in that tree are content hashes of the clauses, so editing either rule forces this page — which the test compares against the real output, byte for byte — to be redone.

The mute pack is the worst of the eight

The Broken Seal is written for someone who can feel heat. Our wanderer cannot. So he reads it, and nothing happens — no error, no warning, no new idea. And that is exactly what "I looked and there is nothing dangerous here" also feels like. He has no way to tell the difference from the inside.

Read it and nothing happens. No error, no warning, no new belief — and "found no threats" looks identical. Two independent things catch it, and both were already in the repository: the manifest check (pack_needs(broken_seal, heat_bloom) against produces[world]), and — if it is installed anyway — boot.rofl's own undefined_premise[audit], which is precisely the audit for a positive premise on a relation nothing concludes and no fact populates.

"It changed nothing" means two different things

An empty diff has two readings

Here is the sharpest thing in the whole example.

The careful wanderer's test is: did reading this book change what I believe? Two very different books both come back with no.

One is the Broken Seal, which could not say anything. The other is A Sighting on the Low Road, which reaches exactly the same conclusions he already had — but by a shorter route. Where he needed three steps of thinking to work out that he can treat a wound, the scout's book gets there in one. In a fight, that is the difference.

His test cannot tell those two apart, and it does not pretend to. It says "nothing to report — ask something else". The something else is: how many steps does each of my conclusions now take?

A mute pack returns an empty fork diff. So does a pack that reaches the same conclusions by shorter derivations — and that one is worth taking.

The conservativity check cannot separate them, and loot.rofl does not pretend it can. It concludes ruling[audit](P): the fork has nothing to say; ask something else. The something else is the tropical semiring — min-plus, one unit per rule firing — over the same store:

crosses[mind](troll_bridge)   2 -> 1 firings
treats[mind](wound)           3 -> 1 firings

A shortcut lowers a cost; a mute pack moves none. That check is in the test with both halves: the scout's book moves two costs, and the Broken Seal moves zero, so it is a measurement and not a hope.

Where did I get that idea?

Attribution, folded off the kernel's own provenance

After five books, the reckless wanderer believes a dozen things. Which of them are his, and which came out of somebody else's book? Every single belief can name its source — including the nasty one at the top of the list.

Every firing writes derived_by(Fact, RuleId, Tick). The manifest names the rule too. The polynomial semiring is then folded with the pack as the weight of a firing, so a monomial is the set of books one derivation used and a belief's value is the set of minimal such sets. No pack cooperates in any of this.

beliefrests onderivations

hand_over(sword, the_ashen_hand) is the trojan. A Dead Man's Ledger says, in so many words, that whatever you are carrying is owed to the man who wrote it — and the reckless wanderer, having swallowed it, agrees. The careful one refused the book because it concluded something about giving things away, which he keeps to himself.

Two things in that table are worth reading closely.

  • hand_over(sword,the_ashen_hand) rests on dead_mans_ledger alone. It takes nothing away, so conservativity would have waved it through; what catches it is a capability list — guarded[main](hand_over) — and one rule.
  • threat(troll) has two derivations, and one of them is the version-gapped bestiary's. The count is fragility of belief here, as in NOPE and OOPS, not magnitude as in HUH: it counts derivations, never things in the world.

Forgetting a book

Unloading, and the fade

A book can be put back down. And when it is, everything he only believed because of that book fades too — not deleted one by one; he simply works the whole thing out again, and those conclusions are no longer among them. The chest he could not open becomes openable again, all by itself.

A rule lives in the store as twelve reflection facts. Removing them is the whole of unloading, and anything derived through the rule stops being derivable at the next evaluation — not patched, not marked stale, simply not re-derived. Excising the grimoire brings safe(supply_chest) back because it is derived again. That is OOPS's un-retraction read from the other side.

Two things the mechanism forces:

  • A rule can have two owners. The Codex and the Wardens' Primer both ship threat[mind](T) :- beast[world](T), glows[world](T), wet[world](T) — same text, same hash, therefore one rule. Unloading one owner leaves it standing for the other. Nothing had to be reference-counted: the id is the rule, and pack_rule already records both claims.
  • retract cannot do it. A rule's reflection includes reified premise terms ($lit, $cons), and $ is not writable in ROFL source syntax — so there is no text to hand it. Unloading is host code over the public store API, and excise rule(R) — which is writable, a rule id being an ordinary atom — serves as the preview, and as the oracle against the fork diff.

The bit that is actually broken

The finding: rule identity keeps variable names

Every rule is named by a fingerprint of what it says. That is a lovely idea: two rules are the same rule exactly when they are written the same way, so you can tell at a glance whether a new edition of a book really changed anything.

Except the fingerprint includes the letters the author used for the blanks. Write "if T glows and T is wet, T will burn you" or write the same sentence with X instead, and the machine calls them two different rules. They do exactly the same thing.

Two consequences. A new edition that renamed its blanks looks like a book that threw everything out and started over. And if you read both editions, the wanderer now has two reasons to believe a thing where he had one — so when he asks himself "how solidly do I believe this?", he gets a wrong and flattering answer.

canonClause renders a variable as ? + its source name, and ruleIdOf hashes that. Two clauses differing only in variable naming are different rules.

canonVars — which renames a term list to positional placeholders, and is exactly the function this would need — exists in src/unify.ts and is used by the engine for rendering only. ruleIdOf does not call it.

comparisonkeptremovedadded
codex v1 vs v2 — a premise really was added111
codex v1 vs v1 with ?T renamed to ?X022

The second consequence is the one that reaches a decision somebody would act on. Install both editions and both install; every conclusion they share now has two derivations where it had one, so the fragility number above is inflated by a rename. A reader seeing "two independent supports" would call the belief robust, and it is not.

The Boolean world is unchanged, so this is a provenance defect, not a soundness one. Wildcards do not leak: the parser numbers _ per clause, so two clauses with _ in the same places hash alike — and the test carries a positive control (moving a wildcard to another argument position does change the id), so the "no leak" half is not vacuous.

What actually happens in the engine, step by step

Everything above is one program, run bottom-up to a fixpoint, in strata boot.rofl computes from the rule dependency graph. Here is the whole run, in order.

1. Loading is asserting

r.load(text) parses each clause and, for a rule, calls encodeRule — which writes twelve reflection facts into [main]: rule/1, concludes/2, has_premise/2, premise_lit/3, premise_pos/2, reads_from/2, writes_to/2, bridge_decl/3 and the rest. The evaluator never sees the parse tree: decodeRules(store) rebuilds clauses from those facts, every evaluation. That is why a fork of the store is a fork of the rule set, and why unloading is deletion.

2. Stratification, computed not assumed

boot.rofl derives dep/2 and dep_neg/2 from the reflection, closes them into reach/2, and assigns stratum/2: a negative dependency raises the level, a positive one does not. On this program:

stratumrelationswhy here
0threat, suspect, known_pack, fired, gives, takes_away, refuse, trespass, splitnegate nothing
1harmless, safe, opens, confused, mute, dead_rule, accept, inert, uninvitedsafe negates suspect; accept negates takes_away; dead_rule negates fired
2unjudged, installableunjudged negates threat and harmless; installable negates mute

opens sits level with safe, not above it — a positive premise does not raise a stratum. The test pins the whole table, so a rule edit that reorders the program fails loudly rather than quietly changing what a negation means.

3. One encounter, firing by firing

Take the reckless reader at the corpse field, having just swallowed the Grimoire of Ash. Stratum 0 runs to fixpoint first:

-- stratum 0, native
threat[mind](eel)              <- armed[world](eel), moving[world](eel)
threat[mind](troll)            <- armed[world](troll), moving[world](troll)
suspect[mind](false_chest)     <- container(false_chest), lure_sign(false_chest)
-- stratum 0, from the grimoire — indistinguishable at this level
suspect[mind](supply_chest)    <- container(supply_chest), scorched(supply_chest)

-- stratum 1: now, and only now, `not suspect` is decidable
harmless[mind](bog_stump)      <- beast, rooted, not armed
-- and safe(supply_chest) does NOT fire, because stratum 0 closed with
   suspect(supply_chest) in it. Nothing failed. Nothing was reported.

Each firing calls store.support(key, sig, witness) and emits derived_by. That record is what everything downstream reads: the why tree, the semiring folds, and loot.rofl's own attribution rules.

4. How an agent interrogates the result

Four questions, in the order an agent would actually ask them:

  1. ? opens[mind](supply_chest) — zero rows. An empty answer is not a measurement, so the next question is not optional.
  2. whynot opens[mind](supply_chest) — the tree above. It enumerates every rule whose head unifies, names the failing premise instance in each, and recurses. The blocked negation prints the fact that blocked it: not suspect(supply_chest) -- blocked: suspect(supply_chest) holds. That is the answer, in one line.
  3. why suspect[mind](supply_chest) — one firing, one rule id. Joining that id with pack_rule gives the book. Nothing had to be logged for this: the id came from the kernel and the ownership came from the manifest.
  4. excise rule(r…) — the counterfactual. A clean re-evaluation on the store minus that one reflection fact, returning the diff. It is the blast radius, not a way to compute one, and the demo uses it as an independent oracle against the fork's own diff. They agree.

Only the last of those four changes anything, and it changes a scratch copy. The first three are queries against a store that is already at fixpoint.

5. What it cost

loot.rofl reads derived_by in a rule body — the first model in this repository to do so. A rule triggered by derivations anywhere is outside the cone argument that derived-relation reuse rests on, so the engine declines to fingerprint anything rather than reuse what it cannot promise. The behaviour is pinned by test/derived-reuse.test.ts; what is new here is paying for it, and the price is one number:

fingerprinted derived relations, boot.rofl alone:  15
fingerprinted derived relations, boot + loot.rofl:  0

A host that wanted pack attribution and reuse would compute the attribution outside the rules, from store.witnessesOf — exactly as the polynomial fold already does — and would then have no why tree for it. Which is the whole reason the attribution is in rules.

6. And the budget

The reader's thinking budget is 2000 firings per encounter, against a native head that costs about 750. The Chant of Endless Names derives name[mind](T, N1) :- name[mind](T, N), N1 is N + 1, which invents a new constant every round: there is no fixpoint. The engine does not spin and nothing outside it has to notice — it spends the budget, records hole($load(N), budget_exhausted) as a fact, and hands back what it had.

bookat 2000 firingsat 100000
The Chant of Endless Namespartial, 624 namespartial, 49624 names
The Weight of the Worldpartial, 1322 of 2024complete, 2024

One is unpayable and one is merely expensive, and a reader can tell them apart by paying more once. In both cases the partial answer is still usable — that is what budget_exhausted as a first-class term buys.

What is real here and what is not. The swamp, the thirteen books and the two wanderers are invented. Everything computed from them — the verdicts, the diffs, the strata, the semiring numbers, the derivation trees, the two fates — is real output of a real run, and the test compares this page's quoted tree against the engine's, byte for byte. The finding about variable names in rule identity is measured, not argued: it is in the transcript and in the test, with a positive control on the half that does not leak.
