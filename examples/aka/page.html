AKAthe seam between two books
A worked example on the ROFL kernel

Two teams wrote down the same customers. They never agreed on the names.

One team keeps a list of accounts — who gets the bill. The other keeps a list of customers — who the company thinks it is selling to. The lists were made by different people at different times and they do not line up: one customer can have two accounts, one account can be shared by two companies, and some names appear on only one list.

So somebody, at some point, sat down and matched them by hand. This tool keeps that matching as a list of who matched what, and how sure they were — and then it follows the money through, shows where the two lists disagree, and points at the money that fits neither.

Two ontologies over one stream of events, not reducible to each other by renaming. Billing has accounts and legal entities; the CRM has customers. The mapping between them is not data either system holds — it is a set of assertions with an author, a confidence and a provenance, and this example treats them as first-class facts subject to every operation an ordinary fact is subject to.

A bridge is not a merge. Two authors may map one account differently and both mappings stand, because their perspectives differ. A conclusion that travelled through a bridge is marked as having crossed a boundary, naming the bridge and whose confidence it borrowed. Withdrawing a bridge transitively marks everything derived through it.

The paired example. Its twin, SUS, shows what happens when two people flatly contradict each other and the tool keeps both stories. This one shows the other half: what happens when somebody tries to stitch two stories together. You need both to see the shape of the idea; either one alone gives a misleading half. examples/sus — perspectives diverge and that is data; examples/aka — perspectives are stitched and the seam stays visible. The metaphor is double entry: several books over the same events are perspectives, and the reconciliations between books are bridges. Without reconciliations double entry does not work.

The two lists, and the hand-made stitches between them

A bridge is not a merge

When two systems disagree, the usual fix is to pick one and overwrite the other. That throws away the only interesting information in the room: who thought what, and how confident they were.

Here every match is a note that says who wrote it and how sure they were. Two people can write down different matches for the same account, and both notes stay. Nothing gets quietly picked.

An explicit mapping with an author and a confidence, itself an assertion that can be disputed, dated and withdrawn. It lives in its author's own ledger, so who wrote it is the load identity checked against authority — never a column somebody could fill in with any name they liked.

-- the integration team's book                   -- finance ops' book
maps[integration](b6, acct_adv_a, adventure, 70).
maps[finance](b7,     acct_adv_a, contoso,   85).

-- both hold. Nothing was ranked, resolved or dropped. What the
-- engine derives from the pair is the finding:
disputed[recon](acct_adv_a, contoso, adventure)
    -> acct_adv_a has two candidate customers, and its money is held back
ShapeWhoWhat it means
corroboratedtwo authors, the same target Independent agreement. Reassuring.
splitone author, two targets The account really does cover both, and the vocabulary cannot say in what proportion.
disputedtwo authors, different targets A genuine disagreement. Both entries stand.

All three are derived, none asserted. And a withdrawal is an entry in the withdrawing author's own book, so finance cannot retract the integration team's mapping without it surfacing as forged[audit] — with no enforcement code anywhere.

A number that crossed a boundary must not look like one that did not

Some of these totals are just addition inside one system — nothing can be wrong with them except the arithmetic. Others only exist because somebody matched two names by hand, and if that person was wrong, the total is wrong. Those two kinds of number should not be printed the same way.

Viterbi over the bridge confidences. A total derived inside one book scores 1.000; a total that crossed bridges scores the product of the confidences it crossed. The independence assumption is stated rather than hidden — the score treats each mapping as right or wrong independently, which is false when one author's whole table rests on the same misunderstanding.

Three different mechanisms produce that table and none of them agrees with the others by construction: the crossed column is written by rules, the certainty is a semiring fold over the recorded support, and the source books come out of the provenance semiring reading the perspective off each base-fact key.

The fold caught the rules. The first draft of the marking rules marked the per-customer totals and forgot total(attributed) and total(ambiguous), which cross bridges just as hard. A hand-written mark can be forgotten; a fold follows the support graph and cannot. The test now pins the two against each other: a total is marked as crossed exactly when its certainty is below 1.
The trap in that table. total(unmapped) — the money that fits neither list — looks like the safest number there. It is the shakiest one: it exists because no match was written down, so every match anybody adds changes it, and nothing on the row can say so. total(unmapped) scores 1.000, crosses nothing, and is filed under within_one_book. It is also the number most sensitive to the bridge set — withdraw one bridge and it moves from $110,000 to $335,000. It rests on the absence of a bridge, finite failure carries no annotation, so no mark can reach it. That is honest, and it is why the tool has to say it in words.

The same number, read in opposite directions

Two of these invoices have two ways of being matched. Reading the number alone, they look identical. They are opposites: for one of them, two different people arrived at the same answer — that is reassuring. For the other, the two ways lead to different customers — that is the problem.

Across this example set the same count means robustness (NOPE, OOPS), fragility (SPAT), launderability (BLEEP), suspicion as a share of worlds (SUS), margin loss (DITTO) — and here ambiguity: more than one mapping path to the same result is a defect, not reassurance. The metric is identical and only the domain fixes its sign. This example sharpens that, because here the domain alone is not enough — the sign is fixed per relation.

The rules derive the classification; the fold supplies the number. Neither is enough alone, and an instance that ships a bare count invites a tool to report robustness where it should report ambiguity.

Not “the data does not match” — a list of missing bridges

When a customer does not add up, the useful answer is not the numbers disagree. It is which match somebody would have to write down, or settle, to make it add up — and the tool works that out from the money.

On a total that does not reconcile: which entities are unmapped, which map ambiguously, and what mapping would have to be added or sharpened for it to reconcile.

$ aka -n tailspin
tailspin does not reconcile. billing says $135,000, crm says $240,000 — short by $105,000.

  the mapping that would close it:
    SHARPEN  acct_wingtip -> tailspin   (bridge b5)
             acct_wingtip holds $105,000 the engine refused to
             attribute, because b5 is not the only candidate: fabrikam also claims it.
             Settling that one ambiguity in tailspin's favour closes the gap exactly.

$ aka -n litware
litware does not reconcile. billing says $0, crm says $88,000 — short by $88,000.

  the mapping that would close it:
    ADD      acct_ghost_ops -> litware
             acct_ghost_ops holds $88,000 that maps to nothing at all,
             and $88,000 is exactly what litware is short. Nobody has
             ever asserted a mapping for this account.
The confident answer is the wrong one. For the reseller account, the money points at b5 — asserted at 0.55 — against b4's 0.60. A tool that resolved ambiguity by picking the higher confidence would have taken the money to the wrong company and reported a clean quarter.

And the two the arithmetic refuses to propose

acct_adv_a's $60,000 has two candidates, at confidence 0.85 and 0.70. Both are refuted: contoso already closes to the dollar, and adventure books nothing at all, so neither can absorb the money without breaking a total that balances.

A confidence is a number somebody chose. The reconciliation is not, and it overrules both of them. A confidence is a number its author chose; the reconciliation is not, and it overrules 0.85 and 0.70. Evidence overruling an assertion, in one rule.

Taking a match back marks everything it touched

Say the integration team realises one of their matches was wrong and takes it back. Everything that was worked out using that match gets flagged — including the reconciliation somebody had already signed off. Nothing is silently rewritten.

Withdrawing a bridge transitively marks everything derived through it — the mechanics examples/oops ships, applied to bridges instead of to retracted papers.

$ integration_team: withdrawn[integration](b2).   -- acct_nw_eu is not northwind

  AT RISK  attribution(inv_03,northwind)      every support gone: b2
  AT RISK  attribution(inv_04,northwind)      every support gone: b2
  SHAKEN   reconciliation(northwind)          gone: b2   still standing: b1
  SHAKEN   total(cust(northwind))             gone: b2   still standing: b1

Withdrawal is history

It was asserted, it was acted on, and it has been taken back. The record that the total once rested on it survives, which is what makes the mark possible at all.

Excision is a counterfactual

What would we believe if this mapping had never been asserted? It produces no flags at all — in that world there is nothing to mark. The tool needs both, and they are not the same operation.

Measured while building this. With the support graph drawn on attributed rather than on link, the withdrawal leaves the flags empty — northwind's total no longer admits it was ever built on the two invoices it just lost. A support that has already vanished cannot be marked: it has taken the record with it. examples/sus states the same lesson about clash versus conflict, and it is worth stating twice, because the wrong version produces a clean empty report rather than an error.

The kernel has a bridge_decl, and it is a different thing

boot.rofl audits cross-ledger flow, and the kernel emits bridge_decl(RuleId, From, To) when a rule's head perspective differs from a premise's. It is real machinery and this example uses it as it stands: six ledgers are read, one is written, every crossing is on the record, and leak[audit] stays empty.

billing -> recon         8 rules      main -> recon      9 rules
crm -> recon             6 rules      sales -> recon     2 rules
finance -> recon         2 rules      integration -> recon  2 rules

-- and an undeclared crossing surfaces without a line of enforcement code:
$ shadow(Inv, A) :- invoice[billing](Inv, A, _, _).
  leak[audit](billing, main)

But it is about rules crossing perspectives, not about entity mappings. It answers “may the reconciliation book read the integration team's book”; it has nothing to say about whether acct_adv_a is adventure or contoso, and it cannot — which account is which customer is not a property of the rule set. One bridge_decl covers the whole of an author's mapping table, whatever is in it.

LicensesGranularityEmitted byDisputable
bridge_decla rule to read a ledger one per rule per source ledgerthe kernel, automaticallyno
maps[X](B,A,C,Conf)one entity to stand for another one per mappingits author, by handyes — that is the point

No parallel machinery was built and nothing was worked around: the kernel's audit is used as it stands, and the data bridges sit on top of it as ordinary domain facts. What the kernel does not supply is the second row, and nothing about the way bridge_decl is emitted suggests it ever could.

Checked against arithmetic that knows nothing about the engine

Both systems report their own totals, so there is a right answer: either the books close or they do not, and by how much. Every one of those judgements was worked out twice — once by the tool, and once by a separate piece of ordinary code that shares nothing with it but the raw ledgers.

Every invoice classification, every per-customer gap, every headline total and the residual identity is decided once by the engine and once by a direct computation over the same base facts in plain TypeScript — no engine, no rules, no shared code beyond reading the ledgers.

billing − crm == the money no bridge can absorb. That identity is the whole claim of the example, and it is checked in three different worlds.

What the oracle does not establish. It shares the definition of the proposal rule with the engine, so it checks that definition's arithmetic and not the definition itself; and it is small enough to check by hand, which is the point of a demo and also the reason none of it is evidence about scale. It is discriminating, and that is tested: hand the oracle a bridge the engine has never seen and the two part company on three separate answers.
