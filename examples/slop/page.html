SLOP — Spreadsheet Ledger Over Provenance

A spreadsheet cell says 0.04732509 and nobody can tell you why. This walks it all the way back — through a total, through a lookup into another sheet — and points at the numbers somebody typed in by hand.

Explained without jargon.

1 A number with no story

A spreadsheet is a grid of boxes. Some boxes have numbers a person typed in. Other boxes have recipes: "add up those twelve boxes", "look this word up in that table and bring back what is next to it". The spreadsheet follows all the recipes and shows you the answers.

Then somebody points at one answer and asks where did that come from? — and the spreadsheet has nothing to say. It kept the answer. It threw away how it got there. If you want to know, you click into one box, read its recipe, click into the next box, read that recipe, and keep going, by hand, sometimes for an hour.

That is not a small annoyance. Spreadsheets run companies, and the mistakes in them are famous and expensive. The mistake is almost never in the arithmetic. It is a number somebody typed into the middle of the grid years ago that nobody has looked at since.

A spreadsheet already is a fixpoint engine. Cells are relations, formulas are rules, and recalculation is iteration to a fixpoint over a dependency graph. It is just a bad one, and the four things it is bad at are one thing:

  • Circular references — a checkbox, a manual iteration limit, no convergence guarantee and no report of whether it converged.
  • Provenance — "trace precedents" draws arrows one level deep, and that is all there is.
  • Uncertainty — not modelled. A point number, or Monte Carlo through an add-in.
  • Sensitivity — retype a value, look at what moved.

All four follow from the carrier of a value being hardwired. A cell holds a double; the engine knows only how to combine doubles; every question that needs a different carrier — a set of sources, an interval, a round index — has nowhere to live.

SLOP reads a real .xlsx, reifies each formula as a tree of facts, and evaluates the tree with rules. The carrier then becomes a parameter: the same tree is walked by three evaluators, and the kernel's recorded support hypergraph answers the rest.

2 The walk back

Here is one real answer from a real spreadsheet, walked all the way back. Read it top to bottom: each indent is one step further into the past of that number.

Every line below is read out of the witness forest the store recorded while deriving the value — not recomputed to draw the picture. Two consequences worth naming: only the branch of an IF that actually fired is here, and the walk goes through the VLOOKUP into the row the lookup hit.

The lines in colour are the whole point. A number a human typed sitting in the middle of a calculated model, and a number typed straight into a recipe where it is not even in a box, are the two commonest ways a spreadsheet goes wrong — and normally you cannot see either of them. A blank box that a total quietly counts as zero is a third.

The two marks are one rule each, over facts the reader emits from the file itself — input(Cell, V) where the file holds a typed-in number, num(Node, V) where it holds a constant inside formula text: 

manual_entry(C) :- input(C, _).
hardcoded(C, V) :- node_in(C, N), num(N, V).

A third mark came out of building it and was kept: a blank cell inside a SUM range is a silent zero in every spreadsheet, and there are twenty of them in the precedent closure of this one cell.

3 Which numbers actually matter

That one answer sits on top of 197 numbers people typed into the grid. But if you changed most of them, the answer would not move at all — they feed other parts of the sheet. Only seven of them can change it.

Normally you find that out by changing a number, looking, changing it back, and doing it again 196 times. Here it is one question.

The provenance semiring, folded over the same support hypergraph, with the base annotation set to name only input facts — so a monomial is a set of typed-in numbers. One fold, no perturbation of anything, and the answer is exact rather than sampled. 

Cost of capital worksheet!K18 rests on 7 manual entries:
  Cost of capital worksheet!H16      Country equity risk premiums!B1
  Cost of capital worksheet!H5       Country equity risk premiums!E189
  Cost of capital worksheet!H6       Country equity risk premiums!E42
  Cost of capital worksheet!I16
complete: true   (of 197 typed-in numbers in the closure)

Two more folds over the same forest answer two more questions. The Boolean semiring, with a chosen input set true and every other typed-in number false, gives the cells that rest entirely on those inputs — ⊗ is AND, so it is "fully determined by", not "touched by". The counting semiring gives, for each value, how many ways the sheet produces it — and here anything but 1 is a defect report, because a well-formed sheet produces each number one way. A count of 2 means a VLOOKUP table holds the key twice, which a spreadsheet resolves silently by taking whichever row it reaches first.

4 How wrong could it be

Every one of those typed-in numbers is a guess about the future. The spreadsheet does not care: it takes each guess as if it were exact and hands back one number to eight decimal places, which looks like certainty and is not.

Say instead: "revenue grows somewhere between 3% and 7% a year — I do not know which." Nothing else changes. No recipe is edited. The answer comes back as a range: 

Input sheet!B26     = 0.05                      the growth rate somebody typed
Valuation output!M3 = 36634.40033821          revenue in year 10

  with growth known only to be between 3% and 7%:

Valuation output!M3 = [28491.05807458 .. 46793.86593094]

That took one pass. Not a thousand simulations, not an add-in — the same walk over the same recipes, carrying two numbers instead of one.

Interval arithmetic lives in the rules (iv/ivalue in slop.rofl), because the carrier of a cell value is the thing this example parameterises. It is declared honestly, and the declaration is checkable:

  • Every input is its value widened by one unit — the rounding the host did turning decimal text into an integer.
  • Every product and quotient is widened outward by four units, covering the at-most-three-unit truncation of the split multiply.
  • Both widenings are outward, so the result is a guaranteed enclosure and not an estimate.
  • A comparison under uncertainty has three answers, and the third is the interesting one: [0, 1] means the model would take either branch, and an IF then returns the hull of both. No point recomputation can produce that.

Because it is an enclosure, it can be tested rather than trusted. Run the interval evaluator with no uncertainty declared at all, and ask whether the double the spreadsheet computed lies inside the interval: 

212 cells checked, 212 enclose Excel's own value
  oracle: AGREE — the interval run encloses Excel's value on 212 cells
Why this is not a semiring. A fold over the support hypergraph computes v(f) = ⊕ over supports of (weight ⊗ ⊗ premises) — one ⊗ for every firing, whatever rule fired it. The hypergraph does not record which arithmetic a firing performed, so a single ⊗ cannot be + at a plus node and × at a times node. The fold answers which, how many, how deep and how trusted. It can never answer what number. (And general interval arithmetic is only sub-distributive over the hull, so it is not a semiring in any case.)

5 The circle

Some sheets bite their own tail. Borrow money, and the interest you owe depends on your average balance over the year. But your closing balance depends on how much interest you paid. And the average depends on the closing balance. Round and round.

This is not exotic — it is how every loan schedule and every model with feedback works. A spreadsheet refuses to compute it until you find a settings checkbox and turn it on. Then it asks you for two numbers you have no way to choose: how many times to go round, and how close is close enough. Then it shows you an answer, and never tells you whether going round more times would have changed it.

A cyclic sheet is not a least fixpoint over a set of facts. It is a numeric fixpoint, and the iterates of a numeric fixpoint are all different facts — so a plain Datalog evaluation of a circular model derives nothing at all. The demo shows that first, because it is the honest starting point.

What a circular reference needs is an index over the iterates. That index is the checkbox, made into data — and once it is data, "did it converge, and when" is a derived fact with a why tree: 

round(0).
round(R) :- round(P), horizon(H), R is P + 1, R <= H.

rvalue(0, C, 0) :- formula(C, _).        -- the seed: every computed cell reads zero
rval(R, N, V)   :- ref(N, C), round(R), P is R - 1, P >= 0, rvalue(P, C, V).

moved(R)        :- rvalue(R, C, V), P is R - 1, P >= 0, rvalue(P, C, W), V != W.
settled(R)      :- round(R), R > 0, not moved(R).
converged_at(R) :- settled(R), not outdone(R).

One rule carries the idea: a reference reads the previous round. Everything else stays inside its round, so the sheet's depth costs rounds and a cycle costs one round per turn round it.

What a spreadsheet gives you

A checkbox. Two numbers to guess. A value in black.

On a model that does not settle, LibreOffice puts an error code in every cell of the cycle:

B8 = #N/A

That is a signal, and it is the whole signal. Not which cell, not how many rounds, not how far it was still moving, not whether anything else had settled.

What SLOP gives you

steady_at      29    stopped moving by more than a cent
relapsed       false
converged_at   (never)  stopped moving AT ALL
still moving   Revolver!C5:
   2946097.81477626

Two answers rather than one, and the residual named to the cell and the unit — here a hundred-millionth of a dollar, a period-two orbit in the truncating carrier.

diverging      true

6 Checked against two spreadsheets

None of this is worth anything if the numbers are wrong. So every number is checked twice, against the two programs that are supposed to be right: the answers Excel itself saved into the file, and a second run of the same file through LibreOffice.

Both oracles are exact and independent. An .xlsx stores, next to each formula, the value Excel computed when it last saved; LibreOffice is driven headless and made to recalculate, and the route is .xlsx → .xlsx rather than CSV so that values come back in full double precision instead of through a number format.

The catch, and it is the point. The oracle is deterministic on acyclic sheets. On cyclic ones a spreadsheet iterates without a convergence guarantee — which is exactly what SLOP claims to fix. So SLOP loses its oracle precisely on the models it exists for. What was done about it instead of papering over it: the converging model is linear in one unknown, so it has a closed form, computed in doubles outside the kernel and outside any spreadsheet — and that check is available only because this particular cycle happens to be linear. On the diverging model there is nothing to compare, so what is checked is the report: both engines fail, and only one of them says why.

7 What it is made of

The reader is node:zlib and nothing else: an .xlsx is a ZIP of XML, so xlsx.ts walks the central directory, inflates the members, and scans the parts it needs — including expanding the 512 shared formulas by translating the master's relative references. revolver.ts writes one too, for the cyclic model, with a CRC-32 and deflateRawSync.

formula.ts is the tokenizer, and its boundary is the same one examples/huh draws: the kernel has no string builtins, so taking IF(D5>0,D5-(D5-C10)*D6,D5) apart is the host's job. Everything above the tree is inference — a why tree bottoms out at input, label, num and the structural facts the tokenizer emitted, never inside the characters of a formula.

The evaluator

value(C, V) :- input(C, V).                              -- a number a human typed
value(C, 0) :- empty(C).                                 -- a blank, read as zero
value(C, V) :- formula(C, N), val(N, V).

val(N, V) :- num(N, V).                                  -- a constant inside a formula
val(N, V) :- ref(N, C), value(C, V).
val(N, V) :- plus(N, A, B), val(A, X), val(B, Y), V is X + Y.
val(N, V) :- pick(N, C, T, _), val(C, X), X != 0, val(T, V).      -- IF, true branch
val(N, V) :- sum_head(N, I), total(I, V).                          -- SUM over a chain
val(N, V) :- find(N, K, T, Col), val(K, Key), tkey(T, Row, Key),
             tcell(T, Row, Col, C), value(C, V).                   -- VLOOKUP

SUM is a chain rather than an aggregate because the kernel has no aggregation: the host lays a range out as sum_item(I, Cell, Next) and the fold is two rules. VLOOKUP reaches the cell, not a value, which is what makes the tree walk on into the rate table.

Money, and where integer-only arithmetic bit

is is integer-only and / truncates, so a value is carried as round(v * 1e8). That scale is a measured requirement, not caution. The first version used 1e-6 and missed by a cent — not through the arithmetic but through representation: this model's operating margin is 0.14063146094259696, which at 1e-6 becomes 0.140631, and a DCF multiplies that 5e-7 error into a revenue of 21765.

Multiplication and division had to be written out, because the obvious X * Y / 100000000 leaves the safe-integer range immediately. Both split their operands: 

X = 1e4*Xh + Xr,  Y = 1e4*Yh + Yr
X*Y/1e8  =  Xh*Yh + Xh*Yr/1e4 + Xr*Yh/1e4 + Xr*Yr/1e8      3 truncations, <= 3 units
X*1e8/Y  =  q0*1e8 + q1*1e6 + q2*1e4 + q3*1e2 + q4         1 truncation,  <= 2 units

The division took three attempts: two levels of radix 1000 handle divisors up to 90 000, and the model divides by a revenue total of 225 471, so thirteen cells silently had no value until the decomposition went to four levels of radix 100. That failure was visible — the cells had no value and whynot named the guard that refused. Which is the difference this whole example is about: the failure mode of a guarded integer carrier is a refusal, and the failure mode of a float is a wrong number in black.

"To the cent" is a property of the model, not of the engine. A unit is 1e-8 of a sheet value. This model is denominated in millions of dollars, so a unit is a cent and the worst cell is 2 units out. The cyclic model is denominated in dollars, so a cent is 1 000 000 units and it agrees with LibreOffice to 1 unit — six decimal places below the cent. Any claim that does not name the unit is not a claim.

8 What it does not do

It reads spreadsheets; it does not let you edit them. It understands adding, subtracting, multiplying, dividing, comparing, totalling, "if this then that", and looking things up in a table — which turns out to cover all but seven formulas in the whole real model. Anything else it says it will not do, by name, and leaves the box empty. It never guesses.

  • VLOOKUP is exact-match only. 108 of the model's 121 calls use the approximate-match form on a text key, which needs an ordering the kernel does not have on strings. Where the key is present the two agree, and both oracles confirm the values; where one were absent, a spreadsheet returns the predecessor row and SLOP returns nothing.
  • The demo runs the precedent closure of one cell — 231 of 1231 formula cells. Reaching fixpoint on that takes ~20 s and the kernel's store insert is superlinear (2004 facts: 20 s; 381 facts: 2 s), so the whole workbook would not finish inside the project's 300-second budget. Not attempted, no performance claim made.
  • The round-indexed evaluator covers arithmetic and references only — no IF, SUM or VLOOKUP. There is no way to parameterise a relation over its carrier, so it is a hand-copy of the point evaluator with a round threaded through. That is the clearest place where the absence of relation-level polymorphism costs something concrete.
  • Three evaluators triple the rule count, and the rule count costs time: 154 rules where huh.rofl is 4, all rescanned every round even when nothing asks them anything.
