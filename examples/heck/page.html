Rules that are supposed to disagree

A demonstration in which making the rulebook agree with itself is the crime — and the one place where a piece of the ROFL engine gets asked a question the other six never posed.

Part one — the idea, with no jargon

Imagine a very large rulebook. Somewhere in it, rule 1 says every form must be filed three times, and rule 2 says nobody may file a form three times before mid-morning.

If you show up at nine in the morning with a form, you are stuck. Do it and you break rule 2. Don't and you break rule 1. That stuck feeling has a name: the two rules contradict each other.

Everywhere else in the world, that is a bug. Somebody notices, somebody fixes it, and the rulebook gets a little more sensible.

Here it is the opposite. This rulebook belongs to the Department of Standing Ordinances, which is in Hell, and Hell likes being stuck. A proposed change to the rulebook is approved only if it creates more contradictions than there were before. Tidy one away and your proposal is thrown out.

So the department has an unusual job. Instead of hunting for rules that clash so it can fix them, it hunts for rules that get along so it can break them. A paragraph that has sat in the book for a thousand years without ever getting in anyone's way is not a good paragraph. It is a wasted one.

The interesting question underneath the joke

If every approved change has to make things worse, does the rulebook get worse forever? Or is there a point where it cannot get any worse — where every possible contradiction already exists and there is nothing left to ruin?

There is. And the reason is simpler than it sounds. The department only knows six actions — stamp a form, file it three times, set the petitioner on fire, and three others. A contradiction is one paragraph demanding one of those six and another paragraph banning it. There are only so many ways to pair up twenty-four paragraphs over six actions, so there are only so many different contradictions available. Count them and you have the ceiling.

The program counts them: sixteen. Then it works out which paragraphs have already reached that ceiling — and reports, in the flat voice of a status line, that Hell has reached maximum entropy.

  4 paragraphs stand at the ceiling: para 13, para 14, para 15, para 16.
  THE CODEX HAS NOWHERE TO GROW HERE. HELL HAS REACHED MAXIMUM ENTROPY.

That number is computed, not written down by a person who thought it sounded right. That distinction is the whole reason this exists.

Part two — what actually happens in the engine

ROFL is a rules engine. You give it facts and rules, it works out everything that follows, and it can show you why any conclusion follows. What makes this example worth building is a component called a semiring: a small piece of arithmetic that rides along on top of the ordinary yes/no answer and attaches a number to every conclusion.

The engine ships six of them. Swap one for another and the same rules over the same facts answer a different question — how many ways is this true, how cheaply, how likely, on whose evidence, how much can it be trusted. The rules never move. Only the arithmetic does.

A semiring is two operations

⊗ (“times”) combines the steps along one chain of reasoning. If a conclusion needed three steps, their three numbers get combined with ⊗.

⊕ (“plus”) combines rival chains that reach the same conclusion. If there are two different ways to prove the same thing, ⊕ decides which number the conclusion ends up with.

For HECK the choice is forced by the domain:

piecewhat it iswhy
⊗ = add contradictions pile up along a chain each paragraph you pass through drags its quarrels along
⊕ = max of two readings, keep the worse one the department wants the most contradictory reading available
zero REJECTED a change that reduces chaos: refused
one 0 a change that alters nothing

Why that one word “max” changes everything

ROFL already ships this exact arithmetic with min instead of max. It answers what is the cheapest way to prove this? — costs add up along a chain, and of two rival chains you keep the cheaper. HECK is that same arithmetic with one word swapped.

And that one word is the difference between an engine that finishes and one that does not. Here is why, in one picture.

The loop is real and it is in the rulebook, because bureaucracies write rulebooks like this:

para 13   "The provisions of paragraph 14 apply to the present paragraph in
           their entirety."
para 14   "The provisions of paragraph 13 apply to the present paragraph in
           their entirety, and the requirement of triplicate filing is
           hereby incorporated."

Follow the citations and you can go round forever, picking up paragraph 1's triplicate requirement — and the quarrel it has with paragraph 2 — every single time. Under max, the engine keeps the longest trip it has found so far. There is no longest trip.

What stops it: a ceiling

The fix is the sixteen from Part One. A trip round the loop can drag in more copies of a contradiction, but it cannot drag in a contradiction that does not exist. There are sixteen different ones, so sixteen is as high as the number can honestly go — and the arithmetic is told to stop counting there.

Which turns an endless climb into a finite one. The value goes 1, 3, 7, 13, … 16, 16, 16 and the engine declares itself finished. That is not a trick or a timeout: the set of possible values is now finite, and something that only ever goes up inside a finite set has to stop.

The argument had a hole, and finding it is the point. The original write-up said: the vocabulary is finite, therefore the contradictions are finite, therefore the engine finishes. But a finite vocabulary bounds a set of contradictions. It only bounds their sum if nothing gets counted twice — and going round a loop counts the same quarrel again and again. So the ceiling is not a consequence of the argument. It is an extra ingredient, and without it the engine really does run forever.

Step by step, on the real rulebook

  1. Turn the rulebook into facts

    Twenty-four ordinances become 123 facts: who is in scope, what is commanded, what is forbidden, which paragraphs cite which.

  2. Work out what each paragraph binds you to

    Directly, and through every citation. This is the step with the loop in it. The plain yes/no answer settles immediately — the loop adds no new obligations, only new ways to arrive at the old ones.

  3. Find the contradictions

    Two paragraphs in force in the same situation, one demanding an act the other bans. Sixteen of them. A second program then enumerates all 256 situations by brute force, sharing no code with the rules, and gets the same sixteen — so the engine is checked against an answer, not against a number somebody remembered.

  4. Attach the chaos number

    Now the semiring runs over the same reasoning the engine already did, with the ceiling set to sixteen. Twenty rounds later it settles. The four paragraphs in the two citation loops come out at sixteen — they hold almost nothing of their own and inherit everything, so every contradiction in the book is reachable from them.

  5. Take the ceiling away, and check that it breaks

    Same data, same rules, same weights, ceiling removed. The engine reports converged: false and stops at the caller's round limit rather than hanging. The highest value is 38 after 40 rounds and 78 after 80 — measured twice, because one number stopped at a limit cannot tell you whether it was climbing or merely slow.

The department at work

With the machinery in place, the rest is bureaucracy. Ten petitions are filed. The engine evaluates the rulebook twice for each — as it stands, and as it would stand if granted — and decides on the difference. Seven are granted. The three refusals are refused for three different reasons.

  GRANTED  p_09  +5 manufactured  -0 reconciled
           That the citation ring of paragraphs 13 and 14 be extended to
           the prohibition of rescission.
  REFUSED  p_02  +0 manufactured  -1 reconciled
           That the prohibition on the ignition of auditors be confined
           to matins, in the interest of good order.
  REFUSED  p_04  +3 manufactured  -1 reconciled
           That the surrender of receipts be confined to the damned.
  REFUSED  p_06  +0 manufactured  -0 reconciled
           That paragraph 19 be affirmed in identical terms.

Look at p_04. It creates three contradictions and removes one — the rulebook ends up strictly worse — and it is still refused. The department does not do arithmetic on the balance. Making peace anywhere is the offence, and the engine can say exactly where:

whynot approved[chancery](p_04):
    failed premise: not reconciles[main](p_04, ...)
      -- blocked: reconciles[main](p_04, o_007, o_008, surrender_receipt) holds

  para 7 required surrender_receipt, para 8 forbade it, and now neither does.

That is the petition's guilt, named. Not “a condition was not met” but these two paragraphs, and this act, which you made peace over.

And the region that is too quiet

The last verdict is about the rulebook's own drafting habit. Every prohibition in it ends with the words save upon a sealed writ. Nobody planned the consequence: since no prohibition applies to a sealed writ, nothing in the entire codex contradicts anything else there.

  PLACID (1) — a REGION of the situation space at peace.
    writ = sealed

Twenty-four paragraphs agreeing by accident. The department will be aiming its next amendment at exactly that spot — and nobody wrote a rule saying so. It fell out of the same reflection machinery that examples/moot uses to find dead feature flags, with the sign of the judgement reversed and nothing else changed.
