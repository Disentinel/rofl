ROFL · example

RIP — Rest In Peace

Somewhere in every online shop there is a list of orders that got stuck. Nobody knows why they are there. This page is about a program that reads the rules of how orders are processed and works out, in advance, which kinds of order will get stuck — and, more surprisingly, which ones can never be rescued no matter how many times you try again.

An AND/OR reachability game over a workflow with non-idempotent stages, solved by the kernel's alternating fixpoint. Three regions: the workflow's winning region, the environment's, and the gap between them — and the gap is not a value the engine assigns, it is the complement of the fixpoint, carrying the firing that put it there.

the problem

The order that paid twice

Retrying a non-idempotent stage is not an edge back to the same state

Here is the whole story in five lines. You order a book. The shop reserves a copy, charges your card, and posts the parcel. Each of those three things is done by asking somebody else — the warehouse, the bank, the courier — and any of them can be slow, broken, or simply silent.

Now the nasty bit. The shop asks the bank to take your money. The bank takes it, and then the reply gets lost on the way back. The shop is still waiting. It waits, gives up, and does the sensible thing: it tries again. And now you have paid twice.

Trying again is only free if doing the thing twice is the same as doing it once. Reserving a copy twice is fine — it is the same copy. Taking money twice is not fine. That single difference is what this whole page is about.

Every retry story assumes repetition is free. Where a stage is idempotent it is: the second call lands on the same state as the first. Where it is not, the retry is an edge into a new state with the side effect accumulated, and the environment gets a move that no ordinary reliability model has — answer after the effect has landed and before anyone has been told.

The machine below is w(At, T, D, R, C, Sh, F): the stage, the attempts spent, whether the workflow is unsure, the reservation, the money that has actually moved, the parcels that have actually gone, and how much misbehaviour the environment has left. The first three are the workflow's own bookkeeping. The next three are the world, and the workflow is not told them.

respond(w(charge,0,0,1,0,0,2), call, ok,      w(ship,  0,0,1,1,0,2)).  -- moved on, no doubt
respond(w(charge,0,0,1,0,0,2), call, fail,    w(charge,1,0,1,0,0,1)).  -- attempt spent, no doubt
respond(w(charge,0,0,1,0,0,2), call, timeout, w(charge,1,1,1,0,0,1)).  -- attempt spent, DOUBT
respond(w(charge,0,0,1,0,0,2), call, lost,    w(charge,1,1,1,1,0,1)).  -- the money moved

The third and fourth lines produce states with the same observation — same stage, same attempt count, same doubt — and different worlds. That indistinguishability is the subject. The fifth behaviour, silence, is the one the whole third category is made of: nothing came back, no attempt was consumed, the environment spent nothing, and the state is a genuine self-loop.

the answer

Three answers, not two

Three regions, and the third is the complement of the fixpoint

The program looks at every situation the order could possibly be in — there are 133 of them — and sorts them into three piles.

133 reachable states, 389 transitions, an environment allowed at most two misbehaviours over the run. Every state falls into exactly one region, and the three cover the machine.

settles 77

Whatever goes wrong, there is something the shop can do that ends with the books straight — either the order arrives and is paid for once, or it is cancelled with the money back and nothing sent.

The workflow's winning region: there exists an action such that all of the environment's answers lead to states that settle. A strategy, not a hope.

doomed 32

The shop has already lost. Nothing it does from here can put things right — the money is gone twice, or the parcel went out and cannot be un-sent. Trying again does not help. This is a bug in the rules, not bad luck.

The environment's winning region. Note what it does not mean: two of these states still have a lucky sequence of answers that gets home. What it means is that a provider which wants it to fail can make it fail.

dead letter 24

Neither. There is no way to guarantee it works, and no way to prove it cannot. It might go fine and it might hang for ever. These are the orders that end up on the stuck list.

Neither region. No strategy forces settlement, no adversary forces failure, and the play can go on for ever. This is not a value the engine assigned — it is what neither of the two limits reached.

All twenty-four of the third pile are in one place: the shop has asked the courier to send the parcel, is not sure whether it went, and the courier has stopped answering. That is it. Everything else about this shop is decidable.

All twenty-four are at ship or track, and every one carries the doubt flag. A dispatch that may already have gone out, and a carrier that has gone quiet. Nothing else in this machine is undecidable, and §5 of the transcript checks that claim from both sides.

how the engine does it

How can a program be sure it is not sure?

Step by step, inside the alternating fixpoint

The clever part is the third pile. It is easy to write a program that says yes or no. It is much harder to write one where "I cannot tell" is a real answer and not just what is left over after the yeses and the noes — because "left over" also contains "the program ran out of time", and those two are not the same thing at all.

Here is the trick, and you can do it on paper. Run the rules twice with two different attitudes.

  1. The generous run. Assume every question you cannot answer yet is answered the way that lets you write down the most. You will write down some things that are not really true.
  2. The mean run. Now assume everything the generous run wrote down. Anything you can still conclude was worth concluding.
  3. Repeat. The generous list keeps shrinking; the mean list keeps growing. Eventually they stop moving.

Whatever is on both lists is true. Whatever is on neither is false — not one single generous run could talk itself into it. And whatever is on the generous list but not the mean one is the third answer: a claim that can only be believed by assuming itself. That is what "it depends on luck" looks like from the inside.

The universal quantifier is the difficulty. "All the environment's answers settle" is a statement about an open set, which a rule language cannot write, so it is written as the negation of an existential:

will_settle(S) :- settled(S).
will_settle(S) :- action(S, A), not risky(S, A).
risky(S, A)    :- respond(S, A, _, S2), not will_settle(S2).

will_settle depends on itself through two negations. That is a genuine negative cycle, and the ordinary phase-ordered evaluator is right to refuse it — the stratum number does not exist inside such a cycle, and boot.rofl's own rule for computing one diverges. Delete semantics(well_founded). from the file and it is refused, at every budget above 2000 steps:

program rejected: unstratified[main](risky), unstratified[main](will_settle)

With the declaration, the evaluator runs two sequences from the same operator, with every not p judged against a frozen set rather than against the store being built. Start from the base layer. A round that assumes little derives much; a round that assumes much derives little. The generous rounds decrease, the mean rounds increase, and here they meet after eight alternations.

  • in both limits → TRUE, the workflow's winning region;
  • in neither → FALSE, and false is a real claim: no round derived it however generous the assumption;
  • in the generous limit only → UNDEFINED — an atom whose only derivation needs to assume itself.

Why this matters. A program that just said "I do not know" would be useless, because it could mean "I gave up". This one can tell you the difference: it can point at the exact sequence of events that goes round in a circle.

The third value is derived, not subtracted. An atom reaches the gap only by being derived in a round, so it carries the firing that derived it. That is what makes why unknown(X) possible and what separates the third value from a budget that ran out — a run cut short reports hole(Id, budget_exhausted) and claims no unknowns at all.

And the undefined set has to move when its cause moves

Take the carrier's silence out of the machine and the whole program becomes two-valued: zero dead letters, zero unknown rows, the negative cycle still there and simply without consequences. Then the other direction, which is the one that could have gone either way — let the payment gateway go quiet as well:

baseline13324130
carrier cannot go quiet12300
the gateway goes quiet too13724130

Four more states and not one more dead letter — the same twenty-four, not merely the same count. Silence alone is not the ingredient. At the charge stage the workflow has somewhere else to go, so the exists-quantifier finds a safe move and never has to look at the silent one; at the shipping stage with the doubt flag up there is no abort, and the probe inherits the same silence. Silence plus no alternative is what makes a dead letter, and the two runs separate the ingredients.

And the same check the other way round: if the courier is forbidden to go quiet, the stuck pile drops to zero. If the bank is allowed to go quiet as well, the stuck pile does not grow at all — because when the bank goes quiet the shop still has something sensible to do, and when the courier goes quiet it does not. Going quiet is only fatal where there is nothing else to try.

the tree

Ask it why, and it draws the circle

why unknown(will_settle(S)) names the unfounded set

This is the program explaining one stuck order. Read it downwards and it is a little story: the courier fails, the courier fails again, the shop has run out of tries so it asks "did you send it or not?" — and the courier says nothing. For ever. The last line is the program noticing it has walked in a circle and stopping.

the useful part

It hands you the recipe for the bug

Best derivation: a reproducing trace, as an executable test

Knowing an order can break is not very useful on its own. What you want is the exact sequence of unlucky events that breaks it, so you can try it yourself. The program has that already, because it kept track of how it reached every situation. The shortest way to make a customer pay twice:

The tropical fold priced every derivation of reached/1. Walking down and choosing at each step the firing whose cost is that number reads the argmin back out of the support the engine recorded — not a search, and not a story told about the answer afterwards.

    step  where                        the workflow   the provider
       1  w(reserve,0,0,0,0,0,2)       call           fail
       2  w(reserve,1,0,0,0,0,1)       call           ok
       3  w(charge,0,0,1,0,0,1)        call           lost
       4  w(charge,1,1,1,1,0,0)        call           ok
       5  w(ship,0,0,1,2,0,0)          call           ok
       6  w(fulfilled,0,0,0,2,1,0)

In words: the warehouse fails once and works on the retry; the bank takes the money and the answer is lost; the shop, thinking nothing happened, charges again; the parcel goes out. The order looks completed. The customer has paid twice and nobody will notice.

That script is pinned line for line by the test and then handed to the simulator, which shares no transition code with the engine:

    replay -> w(fulfilled,0,0,0,2,1,0)   REPRODUCED
    settled? false

And the control that makes "reproduced" a measurement rather than a formality: a script with one answer altered is rejected by the same replay.

the check

Does it actually work? We ran the shop 200 000 times

The simulator, the oracle, and the discrepancies by name

A program that reasons about a shop is only worth trusting if somebody also runs the shop and checks. So there is a second program that plays out orders for real, with a coin flip deciding whether each provider works. Two hundred thousand orders.

The whole machine is re-derived in plain TypeScript from the domain description — a switch over stages, no rules, no engine — and the three regions are computed by the two textbook attractors. Neither is an alternating fixpoint, so the gap between them is an independent prediction.

And the result of the two hundred thousand orders is the most interesting number on this page:

    200 000 random runs -> 173100 settled, 26900 failed, 0 capped.

Not one of them got stuck. That is not a failure of the experiment — it is the whole point. Getting stuck needs the courier to go quiet sixty times in a row, which has a chance of about one in 1040. You could run a billion orders a second until the sun goes out and never see it. But it happens in real shops, because real couriers do not flip coins — they have outages. The reasoning found it without running anything.

silence has weight 8 in 100 and the cap is 60 steps, so a hanging run needs a run of silences of probability around 1e-40. Sampling is the wrong instrument for this pathology. Under the malicious provider — a fixed preference order that knows nothing about the analysis — the same twenty-four states hang on the first run.

Four outcomes, named, never averaged

The comparison is a derived relation in the model, not a diff in TypeScript: the simulator files its journal into its own ledger [sim] and crosscheck/3 joins it against verdict/2. A table of rates would say the two agree 94% of the time and destroy exactly what the comparison is for.

The second row is worth the whole exercise. Replaying each hanging run step by step gives the exact move where the model and the policy part company — eight distinct moves, all the same mistake:

    parts company at              plays   certified
    w(ship,0,0,1,1,0,0)           call    abort
    w(ship,0,0,1,1,0,1)           call    abort
    w(ship,0,0,1,2,0,0)           call    abort
    w(ship,1,0,1,1,0,0)           call    abort

At the shipping stage the policy calls the carrier and the certificate says do not — cancel the order instead, because a carrier that can go quiet cannot be made to deliver. An unwelcome answer, a correct one, and one no amount of retrying discovers.

And when the two disagree, the program does not average them into a percentage. It says which orders, and at which step, and what should have been done instead. An average would throw away the only thing worth knowing.

what a line is worth

One rule, twelve orders

The policy is a block of facts, and each line has a price

Nothing in the reasoning part of the file mentions how many times to retry, or whether to double-check with the bank. Those are separate lines, so you can delete one and see what it cost.

Nothing below the -- @machine marker names a retry count. A policy change is a line, and what it is worth is a number.

Deleting the rule "before charging again, ask the bank whether the first one went through" costs twelve situations their guarantee — and it is exactly the twelve where a retry would take the money twice.

Deleting probe_of(charge, verify) costs twelve states their guarantee, all of them at the charge stage with the doubt flag set. And one line the other way: abort_needs_certainty(reserve) — only compensate when you know where you stand — sounds prudent and costs eight states, because reserving is idempotent and the doubt is harmless there. The policy has no idea which stages are idempotent. The model does.

One thing the program has to be told. If the bank is allowed to be broken for ever, then nothing can ever be guaranteed and the answer is useless. So it is told a number: the providers may misbehave at most twice per order. Every answer above depends on that number, so the program shows what happens at other numbers instead of hiding it.

The fairness assumption, stated as a number because it has to be. Real reliability work assumes providers eventually behave — a liveness property over infinite plays, which a finite AND/OR fixpoint cannot express. slack(N) is the finite approximation, and it is swept rather than asserted.

the honest part

What it cannot do

What could not be modelled

  • It cannot tell you how likely anything is. It says "possible" and "unavoidable", never "probably". For that you need the simulator, and the simulator is blind to the rare cases — which is exactly why both exist.
  • It has no clock. A retry budget stands in for real timeouts, and an order one attempt from the end is treated the same whether that attempt comes in a second or a week.
  • It cannot count past two. Money and parcels are capped, so "charged twice" and "charged five times" are the same state.
  • Somebody has to write the rules down. The program does not read your code and work out what your shop does. That step is a person's job, and everything after it is guaranteed.
  • Fairness. "A provider called infinitely often eventually answers" is a Streett condition over infinite plays. A Datalog AND/OR fixpoint cannot express it; slack(N) is the finite approximation and every verdict is relative to it.
  • Probability. The Viterbi semiring could carry a probability through the machine; it cannot carry one through the alternation, because the third value is not a number. So this cannot rank two dead-letter classes by expected volume.
  • Imperfect information, properly. The fixpoint chooses per state, including states the workflow cannot tell apart. §12 measures whether that mattered here — 23 observation classes, all uniform, zero needing hidden information — and that is a measurement, not a theorem. A machine where the classes split would need a genuine imperfect-information game.
  • Unbounded counters. Capped at two, which is sound for a verdict that only distinguishes "exactly one" and lossy for anything else. dangling/2 is the gate that stops the cap becoming a silent hole, and the demo makes it fire.
  • Time. No clock, no backoff, no deadline. Production dead letter queues are full of tasks that died of a deadline rather than of a counter.
  • Extracting the machine from real code. The transitions are written by hand. The kernel guarantees consequences, not translation — softened here only because the translation is checkable, and checked.

examples/rip/ in the ROFL repository. Everything quoted on this page is demo.ts's stdout, unedited, and test/example-rip.test.ts pins it. Run it: node --experimental-strip-types examples/rip/demo.ts

This is model checking, and no scientific novelty is claimed: well-founded semantics is Van Gelder, Ross and Schlipf's, the alternating fixpoint is Van Gelder's, and games on graphs are a mature field. What is this example's own is the observation that the third value is the dead letter queue — that the tasks a workflow gets stuck in are exactly the ones for which neither outcome is derivable, and that an engine which can say so hands you the class rather than the incident.
