ROFL · example

NPC — the game character that can say why not

Ten little characters live in a walled yard. Each one keeps its own notebook of what it has seen. A computer works out what each of them wants, what it should do about it, and — this is the unusual part — it can always explain why it did that, and why it did not do the obvious thing instead.

Ten agents, a 9×5 grid, four act shapes. Intents are facts, decomposition is rules, the plan is a fixpoint, and conflict is resolved by a Viterbi fold over the support hypergraph rather than by a priority comparison. Then an agent meets an uncovered situation, records it as a fact, and a rule written at runtime is applied to the whole recorded past.

Everybody has their own notebook

A perspective is a ledger, and here there are thirteen

The computer keeps a separate notebook for every character. npc_3's notebook says "npc_5, hurt, over at square (4,5), saw it on turn 0". npc_1's notebook does not say that, because the cart was in the way. Two notebooks can disagree about the same yard and neither of them is wrong — they just saw different things.

Nothing has to stop npc_1 from peeking at npc_3's notebook. It cannot, because they are different notebooks, and that is the whole trick.

saw[npc_3](...) and saw[npc_1](...) are different facts because their perspectives differ, so two agents holding incompatible pictures of the same yard is legal rather than a data error. Nothing enforces the wall between them because the wall is the ledger. An agent that writes into somebody else's journal surfaces as forged[audit] mechanically, with no enforcement code — the test does it on purpose and watches it fire.

One rule writes all ten journals

saw[A](E, K, X, Y, B, T) :- spots[world](A, E, X, Y),
                            kind[world](E, K), band[world](E, B), now[world](T).
saw[P](E, K, X, Y, B, T) @next :- saw[P](E, K, X, Y, B, T).

The head perspective is a variable bound by the body, and an agent's id and the name of its ledger are deliberately the same atom. The carry rule reads and writes the same perspective variable, which until recently surfaced as leak[audit]: the audit recorded every variable perspective as one wildcard, so a rule polymorphic in the ledger looked exactly like a rule reading one ledger and writing another. examples/sus wrote twenty carry rules by hand to get round it. The kernel now records a variable perspective as itself and leak excludes a flow whose two ends are the same term. Ten journals, one line — and leak[audit] is 0, which is the check, not the claim.

Fight or stand still? The computer does arithmetic

Conflict resolved by a semiring, not by a priority if

npc_4 is a guard standing next to a wolf. It has two things it might do. "Drive the wolf off" is a stronger wish than "hold your post" — but driving a wolf off takes two steps of thinking, and each step of thinking costs a little confidence, the way a rumour loses something each time it is passed on.

Every option carries the whole chain of intents it came from. The Viterbi fold multiplies one factor per decomposition step — the priority of the intent that step concluded — so an option's value is the probability of its best derivation, and ⊕ = max picks the better of two routes to the same act. Nothing else in the arbiter is domain knowledge: which derivations exist and what they rest on comes out of the store.

drivepriorityreachesstepsscoreoutcome
repel65strike(npc_8)20.4225chosen
hold_post40hold10.4000—
repel62strike(npc_8)20.3844not chosen
hold_post40hold10.4000chosen

Look at the bottom half. "Drive the wolf off" was 65 and is now 62 — still much more important than "hold your post", which is 40. And yet the guard now stands still. Nothing about which wish is bigger changed. What changed is how far away the doing was.

This is not "65 > 40". Move one constant from 65 to 62 — still far above hold_post, priority order completely unchanged — and the act flips, because two steps of 0.62 are worth less than one of 0.40. No if in any language expresses that, and no priority list contains it: it is a fact about the shape of the derivation, not about the ordering of the constants.

Depth costs, and that is a real semantic commitment. A deeply decomposed plan under a strong drive can lose to a shallow one under a weak drive. A designer has to know that; it is the price of arbitration being an algebra over the whole derivation rather than a comparison of two numbers.

And the inheritance rule is written the awkward way on purpose

The obvious form — intent(A,G,P) :- subgoal(A,Par,G), intent(A,Par,P) — makes the parent intent a premise of the child twice, once directly and once inside the subgoal, and ⊗ over the premises of one firing multiplies its factor in both times: 0.7 becomes 0.343 instead of 0.49. That is not a bug in the semiring. Viterbi folds over derivation trees, and in a tree a shared premise really does occur twice. Reading the constant out of the drive table instead leaves exactly one occurrence.

When the arithmetic cannot decide

Indifference is not conflict, and the difference is recorded

Sometimes two different steps are exactly as good as each other — running left and running right both get you away from the wolf. Then a plain written- down order decides: help somebody, else hit somebody, else move, else stand still. And every single time that order decided anything, it gets written in the log.

The fold scores identical alternatives identically, because they are identical to it. A declared total order over acts then decides, and every occasion it decided anything lands in tie[audit]. It is a real commitment, not a formality: an agent standing beside the ally it means to tend scores tend(B) and move(D) towards B identically, both being one step off the same intent, and without "help before travel" it walks past the wound for ever. That is not hypothetical — it is what the first version of this example did.

"Why didn't it do the obvious thing?"

whynot: the question a behaviour tree cannot answer in principle

This is the question that makes people who build game characters tear their hair out. The usual tools can show you which branch ran. They cannot show you why a different branch did not run, because nothing ever looked at it.

Here you just ask. npc_3 can see a hurt friend two squares away and is not bandaging them. Why not? Because bandaging needs you to be next to the person, and the distance is 4 where it needed to be 1:

You can see which node ran; you cannot see why another did not. Here the demonstration is a query, and it names the premise instance that failed and the value it failed on:

The rule ids in that block are content hashes of npc.rofl's clauses, so editing any of those rules forces the test — and this page — to be redone. whynot takes depth and nodes, and both announce themselves when they fire; the test asks for a demonstration that is too shallow and one that is too narrow and checks that each says which bound cut it. A truncation that does not say it truncated is the failure mode this API is built against.

What one tick actually does

One turn, step by step

  1. publish — the simulator asserts at and hp into [world]. Everything static was carried here by seventeen @next rules.
  2. sense — in_range, screened, spots, then one rule writes a stamped entry into each of the ten journals.
  3. believe — outdated / recalls pick the freshest entry per entity; foe, ally, gap, adjacent, in_sight follow.
  4. intend — drives fire intents, intents decompose into subgoals, subgoals are intents again. Fixpoint.
  5. propose — every intent that can reach an act proposes one, several if several steps qualify.
  6. arbitrate — the host folds Viterbi over the support the kernel recorded, collapses to one score per (agent, act) with ⊕ = max, and asserts score[choice].
  7. decide — rules again: beaten, contender, preempted, does, plus tie[audit] and uncovered[audit].
  8. apply — the simulator moves, strikes, tends. Host code, and the next section says why it has to be.
  9. advance — tickAdvance stages the @next layer, drops the tick-scoped facts, and freezes the tick's provenance.
  1. The yard tells everybody where everything is.
  2. Each character looks around and writes what it can see into its own notebook, with the turn number beside it.
  3. Each character works out what it now believes: where its friends are, where the wolves are, how far away.
  4. Each character works out what it wants, and then what smaller thing it wants in order to get it.
  5. Each wish suggests something to actually do.
  6. The suggestions get scored by the arithmetic from the section above.
  7. The best score wins; ties are broken by a written-down order and the tie is noted; a wish with nothing to do about it is noted too.
  8. The yard makes the chosen things happen — people move, hit, bandage.
  9. The turn ends. Notebooks are kept. Everything else is thrown away and the yard says it all again next turn.

One thing the computer flatly refused to do — until it learned about time

The loop the kernel would not close, and now does

The natural way to build this is to let the rules move people around too, not just decide. The computer would not accept that program at all — and it explained itself instead of crashing or guessing.

The trouble looked like a circle: looking around depends on where everybody is; deciding depends on looking around; moving depends on deciding; and where everybody is depends on moving. Because one of those steps involves the word not ("no better option than this one"), a circle like that has no consistent answer. Saying "but the moving happens next turn" did not help, because the checker looked at which rule depends on which, and not at when.

It looks at when now. A rule that writes into next turn is not part of this turn's circle at all, so the program is accepted — and the world really does move one turn later. Take the word next back off those same two rules and the circle is real again, and the computer refuses again.

Sensing reads at[world]; deliberation reads sensing through not; a transition rule writes at[world]. That used to read as a negative dependency cycle, and @next did not help: stratification was computed from concludes/premise_pos, which carry no temporal marker. The loop was acyclic in time and cyclic in the graph, and the graph was what was checked.

not p now means p is not derivable in the current tick's store. A @next head therefore contributes no same-tick dependency edge — its conclusion is not derived in this tick at all, it is staged and installed at the tick boundary, which is also why it takes stratum 0 there. The kernel emits conclusion_tense(RuleId, now|next) and boot.rofl reads it. The program above loads. What has not changed is the check: take @next off those same rules and the kernel still refuses, with a derivation:

program rejected: unstratified[main](at), unstratified[main](does)
unstratified[main](at)  <= ...
  dep_neg[main](at,moves)  <= ...
  reach[main](moves,at)  <= ...
    ... dep[main](saw,at) ...

The probe carries its own positive control. Acceptance proves nothing on its own — a checker that had simply stopped checking would accept it too. So the control is the same transition one word apart, without @next, which must still be refused; and the same program with no transition at all must load and derive something. An empty result is a fact about the instrument until it is shown to be a fact about the subject.

The simulator here still applies acts in host code and republishes at/hp every tick — now by choice rather than by force, and that boundary is exactly where the fog of war already put it. Deliberation may not read ground truth, and that discipline holds whether or not the kernel would permit otherwise.

"I don't know what to do here"

An uncovered situation is a fact, not a fallback

npc_3 can see a hurt friend two squares away. It wants to help. There is no rule anywhere that says how to walk over to somebody. Most game characters would quietly do something else, or pick at random, and nobody would ever find out. This one writes down "I committed to helping npc_5 and I have no idea how" and then holds its post on purpose.

uncovered[audit](A, no_action, G) :- subgoal[mind](A, _, G),
                                     not any_option[mind](A, G),
                                     not decomposed[mind](A, G).

A subgoal is a commitment to act; a drive that simply does not apply is not a hole, or every agent would be one every tick. hole itself is reserved kernel vocabulary — a rule may read it, never conclude into it — so the domain hole has a domain name, and the kernel's own holes (hole($tick(N), budget_exhausted)) are read separately.

And one of these is left unanswered on purpose. The drifting light is neither a friend nor an enemy — no list mentions it at all — so every guard that sees it writes down "I don't know what this is". Nobody ever writes a rule about it. Seeing one strange thing once is not a good reason to invent a rule about it, and the honest thing is to leave the note sitting there.

The second hole stays open on purpose. uncovered(A, unclassified, npc_10): the wisp is in no hostile row and no kin row. It is never closed. One sighting of one thing is not a reason to write a rule, and the restraint is the same claim as the extension — what the kernel gives you is an honest record of what is not covered; deciding which records deserve a rule is not something a fixpoint does.

The character writes itself a new rule

Self-extension, and the boundary stated plainly

After ten notes of the same shape — "wanted to help somebody, couldn't reach them" — a new rule gets written and handed to the computer while it is running: if you mean to help somebody you cannot reach, walk towards the last place you saw them.

option[mind](A, move(D), tend(B)) :- intent[mind](A, tend(B), _),
                                     step_to[mind](A, D, X2, Y2),
                                     recalls[mind](A, B, _, BX, BY, _, _),
                                     gap[mind](A, B, D0),
                                     D1 is (X2 - BX) * (X2 - BX) + (Y2 - BY) * (Y2 - BY),
                                     D1 < D0.

Be honest about who did what. A person wrote that sentence. The computer noticed that the same gap kept appearing, and it can promise four things about the new rule afterwards: it is stored the same way as every other rule, it applies to everything that already happened, the safety checks judge it just like the rest, and you can diff it. It cannot promise the rule is a good idea.

Choosing that template is a generative step and the kernel does not perform it. proposeRule is a human's template with a threshold of three holes on it, and the threshold is the only mechanical part. What the kernel guarantees is everything after: recorded as facts like any other, applied to the whole past on the next evaluation, judged by boot.rofl's audits, diffable. It does not guarantee the rule is sensible. Same boundary as JOPA: the model proposes, the kernel answers for consequences.

"Applies to everything that already happened" is meant literally. Go back to the very first turn, put the new rule in, and work it all out again — the note that said "I don't know what to do" is gone from turn one, because it was never stored, only ever worked out:

Store.restore comes back dirty, so a restored tick re-derives rather than replaying a log. The hole at tick 1 is not deleted; it simply is not derivable any more:

    uncovered(npc_3, no_action, tend(npc_5)) at tick 1 before: true
    ... and after:                                            false
    options npc_3 now has for tend(npc_5): move(east)

The last line is the one that counts. The friend actually got better. The new rule did not just change what the character was thinking; it changed the yard.

The extension reached the world, not just the store. And the two stalk holes survive untouched — a wolf standing on the exact spot where it last saw prey that is no longer alive, with no step that decreases a distance already zero. The rule was generalised from tend holes and closes tend holes; it does not accidentally fix a different one, and no such claim is made.

Running out of time to think

The frame budget

A game gets a few milliseconds per character per frame. If the thinking is not finished by then, most systems either freeze or hand back nonsense. This one hands back what it got so far, with a note saying it was cut short.

The kernel's inference budget and the domain's frame budget are the same thing, one for one — the rare case where an abstract kernel primitive meets an existing domain requirement exactly. A tick thought from cold costs about 2700 firings here; give it 1200:

    partial: true
    hole(Id, budget_exhausted): $adhoc
    and what it reached anyway:  5 sightings for npc_4, 10 intents, 6 options

A funny catch. The note that says "I ran out of time" cannot be written by the turn that ran out of time — writing it is itself a bit of work, and there was none left. The next turn reads it.

The marker is invisible to the frame that earns it. Deriving thought_partial[audit](Id) :- hole(Id, budget_exhausted) is itself a firing, and there were none left. So the marker is always one evaluation behind: correct for the domain (a frame that ran out cannot also report that it ran out) and worth knowing before designing anything that reacts to its own partiality.

How fast is it? Not very, and here is the number

Throughput, published whatever it is

Ten characters on a small grid see about a hundred new things per turn. But the computer's memory grows by two thousand facts per turn. Almost all of that is the computer writing down, for every single conclusion, which rule produced it and when. That is the whole reason it can answer "why not" three turns later — and it is also the whole reason turn eight costs twice what turn one cost.

The growth is not the yard. Counted by relation, between tick 1 and tick 4:

    derived_by       +5966
    saw              +103
    outdated         +99
    asserted_by      +29
    in_perspective   +29

derived_by is the kernel's own provenance — one fact per firing — and advanceTick freezes it so a completed tick keeps the record of which rule concluded what. That is a deliberate guarantee and it is also the entire cost curve: every fold, the arbiter's included, walks the whole store.

Measured from the other side rather than argued. A second benchmark prunes completed-tick provenance in host code and re-runs the identical eight ticks: 2575 ms against 4571, a factor of 1.8, with both runs ending in provably the same world state — the test checks that, or it would be comparing two different simulations.

The trade, in one sentence. Keeping every reason is the whole point of the system, and keeping every reason is exactly what makes it slow down. A real game would throw the old reasons away, and would then be a game that cannot tell you why its characters did anything.

The trade is the product. A game would prune, and would then be a game that cannot say why its NPC did anything three seconds ago — which is exactly what this example exists to make possible. And anyone reproducing this: measure first, on a cold heap. The identical eight ticks measured at the end of the transcript, after four worlds and sixteen snapshots have been retained, run measurably slower in the same process. The demo measures that too and prints both, rather than asserting the methodology.

Counting across ticks — the third case, and the one that settled it

A carry rule makes every carried fact its own support one tick back; that self-loop met the CLOSED counting semiring, whose star(one) is INFINITE; so past tick 0 the count of anything downstream of memory was "infinitely many". examples/oops measured why, examples/loot avoided it by rebuilding worlds instead of using ticks. NPC could not avoid it — restoring a tick is one of the things it is for — so it was decided instead, the way not p was: the fold is about one tick, and a fact that arrived over the boundary is a given in the tick that reads it. Measured rather than assumed, before and after:

  store                    finite  infinite  on a cycle  in memory
  tick 0, nobody in it       2785       233          26          0
  tick 3, before            9144      1137         186        185
  tick 3, after            10048       233          26          0

The clock no longer moves the last three columns — and the control is the column that did not go to zero: 233 facts stay INFINITE at both ticks, and they are boot.rofl's own reach/stratum closure, a cycle inside one tick. The arbiter still uses Viterbi, which is BOUNDED and converges over exactly the same cycles — a fact the test checks by folding both instances over the same restored store and requiring cyclic > 0, or the comparison would mean nothing.

What this does not do

What could not be modelled

the world transition

The rules cannot move anybody. A normal program does that part.

A negative dependency cycle, refused by the kernel. Not a matter of effort, and any simulation with deliberation in it meets the same wall.

other agents as obstacles

A character will plan to walk into a square somebody is standing in, and the yard just says no.

step_to avoids props it has seen and knows nothing about agents. Four lines to add, left out deliberately: with memories in it an agent would refuse a cell a stale sighting says is occupied — better model, much worse transcript. The refusals are printed, so the gap is visible.

fractions

Health is a whole number. There is no way to say 0.34.

The kernel's is is integer-only, so hp is 0–100 and sight is a squared radius. The spec's own example has hp = 0.34 and there is no way to write it.

real line of sight

"Can I see you" is worked out with a rectangle, not a straight line.

Bounding-box occlusion: four rules, no derived geometry. Coarser than a ray, and chosen against a two-thousand-fact precomputed visibility table. It buys the named blocker, which is what the demonstration needs.

learning anything else

The new rule came out of a fill-in-the-blanks sentence a person wrote. Inventing a genuinely new kind of rule is not something this does.

proposeRule fills one template. Generalising the shape of a rule from holes is program synthesis, and the spec is explicit that the kernel does not do the generative half.

any claim the rule is good

We know it worked here. That is all we know.

Checked by running it and seeing npc_5 healed — evidence about this yard and this roster and nothing more. A rule that helps here and is terrible in general passes every check in this example.
