ROFL · examples/iffy

What if we changed the rule?

There is a rule in force and a pile of decisions already made under it. Someone proposes a change. Which of those decisions would come out differently — and, for each one, exactly which link in the reasoning did it?

Level 1 · the plain version

A rule, a pile of old answers, and a proposed change

Imagine an insurance company. There is a rule book. One rule says: if you want money for a fire, you have to tell us within 30 days. Over the years the company has decided thousands of claims using that book. Some got paid. Some did not.

Now someone says: let us make it 21 days instead of 30.

The obvious question is: what would that have done to all the claims we already decided? Some people who got paid would not have been. But which people? And is it really the 21 days that did it, or something else in the same proposal?

The way this normally gets answered

You change the rule for a few customers, wait, and see who complains. That is called a canary. It is slow, it makes real people the experiment, and it can only ever tell you about the cases that actually came up while you were watching.

The trick

You do not need real customers. You have the facts of every old case written down. So: work out every old answer twice — once using the old rule book, once using the new one — and compare the two lists.

Three things the comparison finds that a canary cannot

Which link did it

Not "43 answers changed" but "this claim changed because the notice rule moved, and that one changed because a different kind of evidence stopped counting."

Two harmless changes that are not

A proposal usually has several parts. Sometimes no single part changes a case, and all of them together do. A canary runs one world and can never see this.

Answers that held on, barely

A claim that was proved two independent ways and is now proved only one way did not change. It is one step from changing, and nothing else would tell you.

And the question backwards

You can also ask it the other way round. Instead of what does my change break, ask what is the smallest change that would give me the answer I want. Try every combination of the proposed clauses — there were sixteen — and take the cheapest one that works. That turns the tool from something that checks a draft into something that helps write one.

One more, and it is the odd one

You can ask: if I deleted this piece of evidence, what falls over? — and get the answer before deleting it. Sometimes the answer is "nothing", and that is still not permission to delete it: the claim it supported may now rest on a single leg where it used to rest on two.

Level 2 · what actually happens in the engine

This is a mode, and it has three applications

The four steps are: fork the thing being changed, evaluate twice over the same facts, diff the two fixpoints, and attribute each row of the difference to a link. Nothing in that says "insurance" or "access control". What varies between applications is only what gets forked.

The last row is the mode's own boundary rather than a difference of taste. DITTO's two sides are two different extractions of two different trees, so its copy is not a design choice — the facts really are different. IFFY's corpus is the same corpus in both arms, so nothing needs copying at all. Whether a fork can be a column or has to be a copy is decided by the application, not by the mode.

Why two corpora and not one

Because one made the mode look complete when it was not. A statute has no norm that defeats a conclusion — jopa.rofl deliberately writes its exclusion as a positive conclusion instead — so two of the four cause kinds below were unreachable and their absence was invisible. Adding the access corpus forced two new rules into the kernel and set off the unexplained audit twice. Shipped on the statute alone, IFFY would have had a quarter of its vocabulary missing and nothing would have said so.

What the second corpus cost: its adapter asks the kernel for 17 rules against the first's 14, while the corpus behind it is three times the size (36 evaluation rules against 12). That near-constant surface is the "mode" claim as a number. The restatement — the corpus's own rules, one column wider — does scale with the corpus, and will not get cheaper with practice.

The fork is a column, not a copy

ROFL stores facts. The obvious way to explore a hypothesis is to copy the store, change the copy, and re-run. The kernel can do that — store.clone() is snapshot() to JSON and restore() back — and it costs a deep copy of every fact in the store. Measured on these corpora: 13.7–18.1 microseconds per fact, which is about 1.6 seconds for a hundred-thousand-fact store, before any re-derivation. That rate is for a store carrying rules, derived facts, provenance and witnesses; a store of bare integer facts clones several times faster and is not what anybody forks.

IFFY does not do that. An arm of the comparison is a term — a name carried as an ordinary column through the rules that read the rule set. The amendment is a delta against the norms already in the store. The entire fork is four rules:

repealed(A, Elem)   :- carries[draft](A, Ed), repeals[draft](Ed, Elem).
enacted_by(A, Elem) :- carries[draft](A, Ed), enacts[draft](Ed, Elem).

in_force(A, Elem)   :- arm[draft](A), enacted_elem(Elem), not repealed(A, Elem).
in_force(A, Elem)   :- enacted_by(A, Elem).

Read the third rule slowly: for every arm, every norm the rule book already carries is in force under that arm, unless that arm repealed it. The norms are stored once. The case files are stored once. Nine arms read them through one rule each. A modification is a repeal plus an enactment, and the kernel needs no third verb — which is also what lets the report say which half of "30 becomes 21" a given case turned on.

The measurement's own honesty

The fact counts are deterministic: identical on every run. The timings are not. The ranges above are three cold runs; across a whole afternoon on the same machine, carrying three other agents, the per-arm figure was measured at 42 ms and at 237 ms. Measured cold it came out negative, because the first world built paid for the runtime compiling the evaluator and the second did not — a fork that makes the program faster is not a measurement of anything. The demo now warms up first, takes the fastest of five runs, and prints "below this machine's noise floor" rather than a number when the difference is not positive.

So the declared ceiling is stated in facts, not seconds: about 100 arms on the statute corpus — roughly 380,000 facts. Everything lives in one store and nothing is released, so memory binds long before the clock does. A full subset lattice is comfortable to 6 clauses (64 arms) and already awkward at 8 (256 arms). Beyond that you need copy-on-write in the store, or a bounded frontier search instead of the whole lattice.

And speed was never the argument. Two fixpoints in two stores can only be compared by a script: the diff has no witnesses, no derivation tree, nothing to ask why of. Two fixpoints in one store make every line of the report below an ordinary derived relation with a proof behind it.

Step by step, on one case

Case g_018: a fire, notice given on day 25, proved by a meteorological report and physical traces. Under the rule book as it stands the claim is paid.

  1. The corpus loads once. 144 case files into the [record] ledger, written by the tribunal of fact. 270 pieces of evidence. Nothing here mentions an arm.
  2. The amendable slice is read out of the statute, not restated. Three rules turn the Act's own tables into eleven enacted_elem facts — peril(fire), notice_limit(30), admits(met_office_report, peril_caused_loss), and so on. Amend the Act's tables and this vocabulary follows without an edit.
  3. The draft arrives in its own ledger. [draft], with authority(draft, drafter). Six amendments, nine arms. An arm asserted by anyone else comes out as forged[audit] from the kernel with no checking code written.
  4. One fixpoint, all arms at once. The statute's rules, restated with an arm column, run over the shared corpus. 48,798 facts, 105 rules, nothing outside range restriction, nothing unstratified.
  5. The control. The baseline arm must reproduce jopa.rofl's own indemnity_due, deliberate_loss and elements_met — computed by rules that know nothing about arms. 47, 42 and 47 rows, identical. An adapter that agrees with nothing is a claim about itself.
  6. The diff is a rule, not a script. lost(A, Q) :- arm(A), baseline(B), verdict(B, Q), not verdict(A, Q). For the bill: 49 of 288 questions flipped, 43 lost and 6 gained.
  7. The cause is a rule too. For g_018:
  under enacted:  q_due  true
  under bill:     q_due  false
  the model says it flipped BECAUSE:
    withdrawn(notice_limit(30))
  attributed to: ed_notice21

  and the kernel, asked directly, names the same link:
    whynot amet[main](bill,g_018,notice_in_time):
      rule r00280332: amet[main](?A,?C,notice_in_time)@now :-
          in_force[main](?A,notice_limit(?Limit))@now, ...
        failed premise: 25 <= 21 [builtin fails]

Two different computations — a model-level attribution and the kernel's own whynot walking the rule body — naming the same link. The test requires them to agree.

Four kinds of cause, because they need four different conversations

causewhat happenedseen in
withdrawnthe old answer leaned on a norm, and the norm is goneboth corpora
overriddenevery premise still stands and a newly enacted norm defeats the conclusionpolicy only
admitteda conclusion that did not hold now does, on a norm the amendment brought inboth corpora
unblockeda conclusion that did not hold now does, because what blocked it is gonepolicy only

The middle two are the ones a tool that diffs supports cannot produce. overridden is a conclusion lost with nothing withdrawn: on the policy corpus, an organization-wide Deny that did not exist before takes write access away from four principals, and every policy statement they ever relied on is still exactly where it was.

The audit that rejected its own author

unexplained[audit](A, Q) derives a flip that nothing explains. It cannot happen while every amendable norm is reachable through enacted_elem — which is precisely why it is worth deriving. It was non-empty twice while this example was being written, and both times it was right:

  • Repealing a permission boundary's allow loses a route whose support never mentioned the boundary — a route survives a boundary by the absence of a deny, so the boundary appears nowhere in the route's provenance.
  • Detaching a service control policy from an organizational unit lifts every Deny it carries while the statement text stays exactly where it was.

Both are now named as blocking elements in their own right. The test deletes one uses rule from the statute adapter and requires the audit to fire, so it stays a gate rather than becoming decoration. Three more gates are planted and required to fire the same way: a repeal of a norm that is not in force, an enactment of one that already is, and an arm carrying two contradictory notice periods at once.

Interaction: two clauses that need each other

The bill for the access model has two clauses. Neither, alone, gives alice write access to the production bucket.

q_access(alice,"s3:PutObject","arn:aws:s3:::prod-bucket/data.csv")

  enacted          false
  a_scp_off        false     detaching the control policy alone: still no Allow at all
  a_grant_reader   false     granting the write alone: the control policy still denies it
  bill_open        true

Five such cases in the statute corpus: a subsidence claim with notice on day 41 needs both the new peril and the longer notice period, and neither amendment alone touches it.

The mirror image is masked — a clause flips a case on its own and the whole bill does not, because another clause hides it. Ten of those. That is the row that detonates later: remove the masking clause in a tidy-up six months on, and the flip arrives with nothing to point at.

Held, and lost its spare

a_no_brigade   fragile   9   hardened   0   supports lost   9   gained   0

Counted structurally: a support carries its own identity in a term, so two independent supports are two facts and the question is whether a second one exists. The test recomputes the same set by set arithmetic in TypeScript, sharing none of the Datalog.

Derivations are not supports

Folded over the same conclusion, the counting semiring says 4 where the structural count says 2. A derivation count multiplies through every rule below the conclusion: two admissible reports each proving causation give two derivations of causation and therefore four of the conclusion that consumes it. Both numbers are correct answers to different questions, and the report prints them side by side rather than quietly using one for the other.

Excise: the radius before the deletion

proposed: delete evidence[record](g_001_e0, fire_brigade_report, g_001, 95)
  radius as an arm, with the fact still in the store: 1   q_due(g_001)
  the kernel's own excise, which deep-copies and re-derives: 1   IDENTICAL
  supports lost without any verdict moving: 0

proposed: delete evidence[record](g_002_e0, fire_brigade_report, g_002, 95)
  radius as an arm, with the fact still in the store: 0   (empty: radius_empty = true)
  the kernel's own excise, which deep-copies and re-derives: 0   IDENTICAL
  supports lost without any verdict moving: 1

An excision arm needs nothing new: it removes a case fact rather than a norm, so the radius is the ordinary lost/2 restricted to that arm. The kernel's own excise deep-copies the store, removes the fact, re-derives from scratch and diffs — sharing no reasoning with the arm at all. The two answers must be the same set, and this is the positive control for the whole example: a radius computed as an arm is a claim about an arm until something outside the arm confirms it.

The second row is the point of computing this beforehand. Nothing decided differently, and a conclusion that stood on two legs now stands on one.

The question backwards

wanted: q_due(g_106)  — subsidence, notice on day 41
  search: 16 arms — the whole subset lattice over 4 clauses — in ONE store
  arms reaching it: 4

  cheapest, by the tropical fold over the support hypergraph:
    cost 5   x06   ed_notice45 + ed_subsidence
    cost 5   x07   ed_notice21 + ed_notice45 + ed_subsidence

  cheapest, by adding up declared costs outside the engine:
    cost 5   x06   ed_notice45 + ed_subsidence
    cost 6   x07   ed_notice21 + ed_notice45 + ed_subsidence

The cost of an edit rides on the base fact that puts it in an arm and on nothing else. Min-plus over the support hypergraph then values each arm at the least total cost of the edits some derivation of the target actually used — which is why arm x07 comes out at 5 by the fold and 6 by the hand count: it carries a clause the derivation never touched. The fold answers "what did this need"; the hand count answers "what does this arm cost". No rule anywhere mentions a cost, exactly as no rule in jopa.rofl mentions a standard of proof. The number belongs to the fold.

What could not be modelled

  • An amendment that changes a rule. IFFY forks the rule-set data a corpus exposes. Rewriting a rule is a new statute, not an amendment, and nothing here detects the difference. A corpus whose norms live in rules rather than facts cannot be forked this way at all.
  • The cost of threading the column. An adapter restates its corpus's evaluation rules with one extra column — 8 of the statute's 12, 16 of the policy's 36. The restatement is checked against the original, but it is a restatement, and if the original moves it has to follow.
  • A slot with at most one value. The element vocabulary is flat, so an arm carrying two amendments that each set the notice period has both in force and the longer wins silently. The constraint has to live in each adapter; nothing checks that its author wrote one.
  • A repeal that causes a gain, in the tropical fold. Costs ride on enactments, so a gain produced by removing a blocker is priced at zero. The statute corpus has no such path; the policy corpus does.
  • Anything about whether the amendment is a good idea. IFFY says which past decisions move and on which link. Whether the new answers are better is a question about the world.
